What Is the Ontario Literacy Requirement? All the Ways Your Child Can Meet It

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 26, 2026
  • 6 Min

The OSSLT, the Literacy Course, and What Happens If the First Try Does Not Work Out

If your child is in Grade 10, you may have heard a phrase that sounds more complicated than it is: the Ontario literacy requirement. In plain language, it means that to earn an Ontario Secondary School Diploma (OSSD), a student must show they can read and write at the level the province expects by the end of Grade 9. It sits alongside the other diploma requirements, including earning credits and completing community involvement hours. The good news is that there is more than one way to meet it. Passing the OSSLT test is the most common route, but it is not the only one. Here is a calm, clear look at every path, so your family knows exactly where your child stands.

1. What the literacy requirement actually is

The literacy requirement is a graduation requirement set by the Ontario Ministry of Education. Students demonstrate the reading and writing skills described in the Ontario curriculum up to the end of Grade 9 — skills they use across subjects, not just in English class. That means reading a news report and understanding what it actually says, following an argument, and writing clearly enough that a reader can follow the main idea. It is not a test of obscure vocabulary or speed. It is a check that a student can handle the everyday reading and writing that high school, work, and life after school demand. Most students meet the requirement in Grade 10, and schools will let families know which route their child is on.

2. Path 1: Pass the OSSLT

The most common way to meet the requirement is to pass the Ontario Secondary School Literacy Test, the OSSLT, run by EQAO. The test is usually written in Grade 10 and covers reading and writing based on the curriculum up to the end of Grade 9. On the reading side, students work with information paragraphs, news reports, dialogues, real-life narratives, and graphic texts such as charts. On the writing side, they complete tasks including a news report based on a headline and picture, an opinion piece of several paragraphs, and short written responses. This school year there are two administration windows: November 3 to 30, 2026, and March 30 to April 19, 2027. Your child's school will tell you which window applies and on which days the test is written. A student who passes has met the literacy requirement, and that part of graduation is done.

3. Path 2: Take the Ontario Secondary School Literacy Course (OLC4O)

The second route is a course, not a test: the Ontario Secondary School Literacy Course, known by its course code OLC4O. A student who has not been successful on the OSSLT, or who is eligible to take the course instead, can earn the literacy requirement by passing OLC4O. The course teaches the same reading and writing skills the test measures — reading for meaning, organizing ideas, and writing with clear structure and correct conventions — but in a classroom setting, over a semester, with a teacher, rather than in one test sitting. If your child is considering this route, talk to the school's guidance department about when OLC4O is offered and whether your child is eligible to enrol.

4. What if neither route has worked yet?

If your child has written the OSSLT and not been successful, the first thing to know is that this does not block graduation on its own. Students can write the test again in a later administration window, or they can take OLC4O. There is also an adjudication process in specific circumstances, where a school can apply on a student's behalf — the school decides whether it applies, so the right move is to ask the guidance department or principal directly rather than guessing. What matters most is that you do not let a disappointing result sit unaddressed for a year. Ask the school for the result details, find out which skills were weakest, and make a plan for the next attempt or for the course. A clear next step, taken promptly, is worth far more than worrying quietly about it.

5. How to prepare, whichever route your child takes

Whichever path your child is on, the preparation is the same, because the skills are the same. Read something every day — articles, short stories, news pieces — and practise saying the main idea in one sentence, with one detail that proves it. For writing, practise the two formats that matter most: a short news report that answers who, what, where, when, why, and how, and an opinion piece with a clear opening position, a few reasons each developed with specific details, and a conclusion that restates the position. EQAO publishes sample test materials, and working through one under timed conditions is excellent preparation for the test route. Before test day, keep the basics in place: normal sleep, a real breakfast, and a calm morning.

6. How tutoring can help

If your child's report card or teacher comments mention vague issues like 'develop your ideas more' or 'check your conventions,' targeted help can turn those comments into a concrete plan. An Ontario-certified teacher can work through the OSSLT reading and writing formats until they feel familiar, practise the news report and opinion structures until they become automatic, and give feedback the way the test's scoring guides do — on main idea, organization, and language conventions. A few focused sessions before a test window, or steady support through OLC4O, can take the fear out of the requirement. If the first attempt did not go well, a tutor can rebuild the weakest skill step by step, so the second attempt — or the course — starts stronger.

The Ontario literacy requirement can sound intimidating until you see it for what it is: one graduation requirement, with more than one way to meet it. Whether your child passes the OSSLT, completes the literacy course, or needs a second attempt with a clearer plan, the requirement is reachable — and the skills behind it are the same reading and writing skills your child will use long after graduation. If you are unsure which route your child is on, the school's guidance department is the best first call.
