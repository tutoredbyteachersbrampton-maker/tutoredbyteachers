Is My Child Behind? How to Spot Learning Gaps Early in the School Year

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • September 26, 2026
  • 6 Min

The Early Signs That Matter, Why Fall Is the Best Time to Act, and What to Do Next

It is one of the most common questions Brampton parents ask me: "My child's grades look okay, but something feels off — are they actually behind?" As a teacher, I will tell you straight: marks in September and October can hide a lot. The first units of the year are often review, homework is lighter, and a child can cruise for weeks on last year's skills. A learning gap — a missing piece of knowledge or skill that everything new is supposed to build on — rarely shows up on a report card until it has already grown. Here is how to spot one early, while it is still small and fixable.

1. Marks are not the whole story — watch for effort that doesn't pay off

The biggest red flag I see is not a low grade. It is a child who works hard and still struggles. If your child sits down, tries, spends the evening on homework, and comes home with confusion anyway, something underneath is missing. Other signs to watch for: homework that takes far longer than it should, constant erasing or starting over, and frustration that arrives faster than it used to. A child who says "I'm just bad at math" or "I hate reading" is usually telling you about a gap, not a personality trait. Pay special attention to avoidance — the child who "forgot" their homework three nights in a row is often avoiding the feeling of not understanding it.

2. Skills stack — and that is why gaps get worse with time

School subjects are built like towers. In math, a Grade 5 student who never quite got fractions will hit a wall in Grade 6 when those fractions turn into ratios and percentages. A student shaky on multiplication facts will drown in Grade 7 when multi-step problems assume those facts are automatic. Reading works the same way: a child who reads slowly and painfully will avoid reading, and avoiding reading means they stop building vocabulary — which then makes every subject, from science to history, harder. This is the real reason teachers push parents to act in the fall. A gap that takes four weeks to fix in October can take four months to fix by February, because by then the whole class has moved two units past it.

3. The subject-specific signs teachers look for

In math, watch for a child who counts on their fingers well past the age when classmates don't, who guesses instead of working through a problem, or who gets the right answer in class but cannot repeat it the next day — that means the method never stuck. In reading and writing, watch for a child who avoids reading aloud, who cannot tell you what a chapter was about in their own words, or whose writing is stuck at the same level it was a year ago. Spelling is a quiet signal too: a child whose spelling is not improving at all from year to year is often missing the sound-and-pattern skills that phonics instruction was supposed to build. None of these means your child is "behind" for life. Each of them points to a specific, fixable skill.

4. Use the fall progress report as a diagnostic, not a verdict

Ontario elementary schools send home progress reports in October and November. These are not report cards — they do not have letter grades or percentage marks. Instead, teachers describe progress toward the curriculum expectations and flag areas needing attention. This makes them incredibly useful for spotting gaps: a comment like "is working toward understanding of fractions" or "needs to practise reading strategies" is the teacher telling you exactly where the missing piece is. Read the learning skills section closely too. Comments about organization, independence, or initiative often point to a child who is lost but coping quietly. If anything in the report is unclear, fall parent-teacher interviews are the perfect time to ask: "What is the one skill my child most needs to build this year?"

5. Compare against the curriculum, not against other kids

Parents often ask how their child compares to classmates. That is the wrong yardstick — what matters is whether your child is meeting the Ontario curriculum expectations for their grade, which are public and easy to check on the Ministry of Education website. Ask your child's teacher which expectations are the focus this term, and look at your child's work with those in mind. Is a Grade 4 student reading grade-level texts fluently? Is a Grade 7 student solving multi-step problems independently? Is a Grade 9 student showing the algebraic thinking the de-streamed Grade 9 math course expects? If the answer is no on a skill that this year's work builds on, that is your gap — and naming it precisely is the first step to closing it.

6. What you can do at home right now

Start small and targeted. Pick one gap — the one skill that keeps tripping your child up — and spend fifteen focused minutes a day on it. Five-minute math fact drills, reading one short article together and talking about it, or writing one solid paragraph a few times a week all work better than two exhausted hours on a Sunday. Tie practice to real life: cooking for fractions, grocery flyers for budgeting, the news for reading and discussion. And protect the basics that feed learning — a consistent bedtime, a real breakfast, and homework at the same time each day. None of this replaces fixing the actual gap, but it makes everything else work better. If you try for a few weeks and the same skill still will not stick, that is a sign your child needs someone to teach it differently, not just more of the same.

7. How tutoring can help

The hardest part of a learning gap is finding it. An Ontario-certified teacher can assess exactly where your child's understanding breaks down — is it fractions, or is it actually the multiplication underneath the fractions? — and then teach that missing piece directly, one-on-one or in a small group of no more than four students. Because fall is when gaps are smallest, even a short run of sessions in October or November can get a child caught up before the winter report cards. And if the gap is in reading or writing, getting it addressed now pays off across every subject, not just English.

"Behind" is not a label — it is a location, and locations can change. The parents who get the best results are the ones who act in the fall, when the gap is one missing piece and not a whole crumbling tower. Trust what you are seeing at the kitchen table, check it against the teacher's progress report, and move early. Your child does not need to be perfect. They just need the foundation solid under them.
