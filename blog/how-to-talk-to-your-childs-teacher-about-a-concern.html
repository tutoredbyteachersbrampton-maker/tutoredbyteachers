How to Talk to Your Child's Teacher About a Concern (Without the Awkwardness)

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • November 20, 2026
  • 6 Min

What to Say, When to Say It, and How to Keep the Relationship Strong

Most parents have been there. Something about your child's school day isn't sitting right — a mark that dropped, a friendship that went sideways, a homework battle every single night — and the thought of raising it with the teacher makes your stomach tighten. Will it sound like a complaint? Will it somehow make things worse for your child? As a teacher, I would much rather hear from you early, calmly, and directly than find out about a problem months later. This is how to have that conversation — and actually get somewhere with it.

1. Teachers actually want to hear from you

Start with the fact that surprises most parents: a parent raising a concern is not a problem for a teacher — it is useful information. Teachers see your child for a few hours a day in a room full of other children. You see them at breakfast, at homework time, and at bedtime — and when those two pictures do not match, the teacher needs your half of the story. The parents teachers worry about are the ones who stay silent until the progress report or the end of the year, when a small fix could have been made months earlier.

2. Start with what you've noticed, not what you've decided

The single biggest difference between a conversation that goes well and one that goes sideways is where it starts. "My daughter is struggling with math and I think she needs more help" opens a door. "The teacher isn't teaching her properly" slams it. Before you reach out, write down two or three specific things you have actually seen: "He used to finish his math homework in twenty minutes and now it takes an hour and ends in tears," or "She has stopped wanting to go to school on Tuesdays." Specific observations give the teacher something real to respond to, instead of putting them on the defensive before the conversation has even started.

3. Pick the right moment — and the right channel

A quick chat at drop-off is fine for a small question, but not for a real concern — teachers are on duty and cannot give you their full attention in a crowded hallway. The best first step is a short, polite email or a message through the school's usual channel: introduce yourself, name the concern in one or two sentences, and ask for a time to talk — in person or by phone. If your school has parent-teacher interviews coming up, that is a natural moment too, but don't wait for one if the concern is pressing. Fall progress reports go home in October and November, and that season is exactly when teachers expect these conversations.

4. A simple script that works

If you are not sure what to say when the meeting starts, try this shape: what you have noticed, how your child feels about it, and what you would like to figure out together. "I've noticed Sarah is spending a lot longer on her reading homework and says she doesn't understand the passages. She feels embarrassed about it. I wanted to check what you're seeing in class and whether there's something we can try at home." Then stop talking and listen. Ask what the teacher is seeing and what they suggest. End by agreeing on one concrete next step and a time to check back in — even a quick email in two weeks.

5. When the concern is bigger: bullying, marks, or learning gaps

Some concerns need more than a conversation. If your child is being bullied or feels unsafe, say so plainly and in writing, with dates and details, and ask what the school's plan is. If marks have dropped suddenly, ask to see the work behind the mark: a single bad test and a steady slide are very different problems. And if you suspect a learning gap — fractions never really clicked, or reading has always been a struggle — say it directly. Teachers can arrange extra support at school, and a short assessment can tell you whether the issue is one skill or something broader. The earlier these conversations happen, the more options there are.

6. If the first conversation doesn't fix it

Most concerns are sorted out in one good conversation. But if nothing changes, don't give up — escalate calmly and in order. First, follow up with the teacher and reference the plan you agreed on. If that still doesn't work, the next step is the school's vice-principal or principal. Bring the same notes: what you observed, what was agreed, and what happened since. Keep every exchange respectful and in writing where it matters. A calm parent with clear notes is taken seriously at every level of a school — and your child sees you advocating for them without turning it into a fight.

7. How tutoring can help

Sometimes the conversation with the teacher confirms what you suspected: your child needs more one-on-one time than a classroom of thirty can give. That is not a failure — of the teacher, or of your child. It is exactly the gap tutoring exists to fill. An Ontario-certified teacher can work on the specific skill behind the concern — reading comprehension, fractions, or study habits — and report back to you in plain language. One-on-one sessions are $40 an hour, and small groups of up to four students are $25 an hour per student, online or in person in Brampton. A free trial session is a good way to find out whether a little extra help would take the worry off the table.

Talking to your child's teacher about a concern is not a confrontation — it is the two adults who know your child best comparing notes. Go in with specifics instead of conclusions, listen as much as you speak, and leave with a plan. Your child will be better off for it, and so will the relationship with their teacher.
