How Much Sleep Does My Child Need to Do Well in School?

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 17, 2026
  • 6 Min

The Bedtime–Brain Connection: Why Sleep Is Your Child's Best Study Tool

If homework has started taking twice as long as it should, the first thing I ask parents is not about study habits — it's about bedtime. As a Grade 7 teacher in Brampton, I see the same pattern every fall: bright kids running on empty, and parents who don't realize that lost sleep can undo an evening of perfectly good studying. Sleep is not downtime for the brain. It is when the brain does its most important schoolwork.

1. What sleep actually does for a learner

During the day, your child's brain collects a flood of new information — math facts, spelling words, science vocabulary. It is during sleep that the brain sorts, strengthens, and files all of it away. Deep sleep locks in facts and skills; the dream-heavy REM stage supports creativity and problem-solving. A tired brain also struggles with what school demands most: attention and working memory. That is why a child who "studied for hours" but slept badly often blanks on test day — the material never got filed properly overnight. Sleep does not just rest the learner; it finishes the learning.

2. How much sleep kids actually need

Pediatric guidelines are clear, and they surprise most parents. School-aged children from about 6 to 12 need roughly 9 to 12 hours of sleep a night; teenagers from about 13 to 18 need roughly 8 to 10. Most families fall well short — a Grade 6 student getting eight hours is already running a deficit. One thing worth knowing: a teenager's internal clock naturally shifts later, so your teen is not lazy for struggling with a 6:30 a.m. wake-up. Biology is working against them, which makes an early, consistent bedtime even more important in the teen years.

3. The signs it's sleep, not school trouble

Tiredness does not always look like yawning. Watch for homework that drags on forever, careless mistakes in work your child normally handles easily, a short fuse in the evening, a Monday-morning battle to get up, and a general "I don't care" attitude toward school. I have seen many kids brought in for tutoring because of "learning gaps" that turned out, at least partly, to be sleep gaps. If your child can still perform when rested and fresh, poor sleep is a strong suspect — rule out the simple, fixable causes before assuming a bigger academic problem.

4. The screen problem — and what to do about it

The biggest bedtime enemy in most homes is a glowing rectangle. The light from phones, tablets, and TVs signals the brain to stay alert and delays the release of melatonin, the hormone that makes us feel sleepy. A child scrolling until lights-out is asking their brain to fall asleep on command right after being told to stay awake. The fix that works best: devices charge overnight in a parent's room, not the child's. Aim for no screens in the last 30 to 60 minutes before bed, and swap the scroll for reading — even 20 minutes helps the brain downshift. For teens, this works far better as a negotiated agreement ("phones downstairs by 10") than as an announcement handed down without discussion.

5. A bedtime routine that actually works

The secret to good sleep is boring consistency. Pick a bedtime and keep it — even on weekends, within about an hour. A big weekend sleep shift confuses the body's clock the same way travel does, which is why Monday mornings feel awful. Build a 20 to 30-minute wind-down: bath or shower, pajamas, dim the lights, read or talk about the day. For younger kids, a visual bedtime chart they can follow themselves turns the routine into something they own. For teens, the negotiation matters more than the number: explain the why. When a teenager understands that sleep protects their marks — and that progress-report season in October and November is when focus counts most — the 10 p.m. lights-out feels less like punishment and more like a strategy.

6. On test day, sleep beats cramming

Here is the most practical thing I tell families: the night before a big test, sleep wins. A rested student recalls more than a student who stayed up an extra hour re-reading notes. This matters most at the times Ontario parents already have circled — the OSSLT windows (November 3 to 30, 2026 and March 30 to April 19, 2027) and the EQAO tests in Grades 3, 6, and 9 (May 4 to June 8, 2027 for the elementary grades). The plan for those nights: stop studying by early evening, eat a proper dinner, and get to bed at a normal time. A rested brain reading each question carefully beats a tired brain that studied one more hour but can't think straight.

7. How tutoring can help

You might wonder what tutoring has to do with sleep. More than you would think. When a student works with an Ontario-certified teacher during the day — homework done efficiently, concepts explained clearly, study time productive — evenings stop stretching into late-night panic. A calmer evening makes a normal bedtime achievable, and a normal bedtime makes the next day's learning stick. We also notice patterns parents miss: if a student is consistently unfocused or exhausted in sessions, we will say so, because sometimes the barrier to better marks is not academic at all. And if the real issue is a genuine learning gap, that is exactly what our one-on-one sessions (CA$40/hr) and small groups of up to four (CA$25/hr per student) are built to fix — always starting with a free trial session.

Before you buy new study guides or rearrange the homework routine, take an honest look at bedtime. Enough sleep will not turn a struggling student into a straight-A student overnight — but a well-rested brain learns faster, remembers more, and handles stress better. Those are the foundations everything else is built on, and the most powerful study tool your child owns is already in their bedroom. Help them use it.
