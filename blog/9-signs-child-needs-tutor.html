9 Signs Your Child Needs a Tutor (Before Report Cards Tell You)

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • September 23, 2026
  • 6 Min

A Certified Ontario Teacher's Checklist for Brampton Parents

There is a moment almost every parent has: you are looking at a report card, or a test paper, or a defeated little face across the kitchen table — and you think, "How did I not see this coming?" If that thought has crossed your mind, this article is for you. As a certified Ontario teacher working with Brampton families every day, I see the same early warning signs again and again. Most parents miss them, not because they do not care, but because they do not know what to look for. And the hardest truth is this: learning gaps do not sit still while you wait. They compound, quietly, week after week. Here is my checklist — nine signs I tell every parent to watch for, and what each one really means.

1. Homework has turned into a nightly battle

Every child has an off night. But when homework takes two hours for thirty minutes of work, when it ends in tears, shouting, or stalling — that is not laziness. It is a child who lacks the tools for what is being asked. Watch for avoidance too: the "forgotten" textbook, the sudden snack break, the fifteen-minute bathroom trip. Children avoid work that makes them feel incompetent. If homework has become a nightly battle, something underneath needs attention — and the longer you wait, the deeper it runs. If this sounds like your evenings, a free assessment can pinpoint exactly which skills your child is missing.

2. Your child has started saying "I'm just bad at math"

Listen to how your child talks about school. There is a world of difference between "this is hard" and "I'm dumb at this." The first is a student facing a challenge; the second is building their identity around failure. Once a child decides they are "bad at math," they stop trying — and when effort drops, the gap widens faster. This confidence crash is one of the most common things I see in my classroom, and one of the most reversible. A certified teacher who meets a child at their level and hands them a few real wins can undo months of "I'm bad at this" fast. If your child's self-talk has turned negative, act now — not after the next report card.

3. They are missing key Ontario curriculum milestones

The Ontario curriculum builds layer on layer, so a missed foundation grows into a bigger problem every year. The milestones I watch most closely: by the end of Grade 3, your child should read fluently — Grades 1 to 3 teach reading, and from Grade 4 on, reading is assumed in every other subject. By Grades 4 to 5, multiplication and division facts should be solid. In Grades 5 to 8, fractions, decimals, and percents are the most common compounding gap I see — shaky fractions in Grade 6 become a crisis in Grade 8 algebra. And in writing, students should organize ideas into clear paragraphs by Grade 5 and structured multi-paragraph pieces by Grades 7 and 8. If several of these feel uncertain, a free assessment can tell you exactly where your child stands against Ontario expectations.

4. Grades are slipping — or the teacher's comments are

Report cards are a lagging indicator: by the time a grade drops, the gap behind it has usually been growing for months. Watch for earlier signals — a slide from A to B in a subject that used to be easy, test marks trending down, or comments like "would benefit from extra practice." And please, do not carry guilt about this. Parents are not expected to diagnose curriculum gaps; you are expected to notice the pattern and get help, the same way you would with a dentist. If the grades are moving the wrong way, an Ontario-certified teacher can find where the slide started and rebuild from there.

5. The teacher is dropping hints

Teachers rarely say "your child is failing" at a parent-teacher interview. You will hear phrases like "could be doing better" or "needs to apply themselves" instead. That is teacher code for: your child is capable of more, but something is getting in the way. Sometimes it is a real skill gap; sometimes a confidence problem; sometimes the child has checked out because the class pace does not suit them. Whatever the cause, that comment is an invitation to look closer. Ask one direct question: "What is the one skill my child most needs to strengthen?" If the answer surprises you — or you are not sure what to do with it — a free trial session with a certified teacher can help you figure that out.

6. Your child is bored — not struggling

Let us clear up a myth: tutoring is not only for kids who are failing. Some of the students who benefit most are the ones who are bored. If your child finishes work in half the time and then zones out, complains school is "too easy," or coasts without effort, they are not being served by more of the same. A bored child often looks like a lazy child on paper — declining effort, careless mistakes — but the fix is the opposite of remediation: enrichment, challenge, and work that actually engages them. Ontario-certified teachers can stretch a capable student well beyond what a packed classroom allows. If your child is under-challenged rather than behind, tutoring is still the answer — just a different kind of it.

7. You are seeing avoidance, guessing, and copying

Watch what your child does when they do not know an answer. A confident learner tries, gets it wrong, and adjusts. A struggling learner guesses wildly, copies a friend's answer, "loses" the worksheet, or suddenly has a headache on test day. These are coping strategies — and they mean your child has decided that trying honestly is worse than hiding. The danger is that avoidance works in the short term: tonight's homework gets done while the gap underneath quietly grows. If you are seeing patterns like this, resist punishing the behaviour and address the cause instead. A free assessment gives your child a low-pressure setting to show what they actually know — and where they have been quietly guessing.

8. The gap is compounding — and you can feel it

This is the sign most parents feel but cannot name: each new topic seems harder than the last, and it never really gets easier. That is compounding in action. Math is the clearest example — fractions feed into ratios, ratios feed into algebra. But it happens in reading and writing too: a child who reads slowly avoids reading, reads less, and falls further behind every term. The good news is that compounding works in reverse as well — fix the foundation, and each repaired skill makes the next topic easier. The question is never whether your child can catch up; it is how much longer the gap is allowed to grow. If this section hit home, you have your answer.

9. If several of these ring true, here is what to do next

Count them up. If two or three of these signs sound familiar, your instinct is probably right — and the most useful next step is information, not more worrying. A free assessment session with an Ontario-certified teacher pinpoints exactly where the gaps are, so you are acting on facts instead of fear. At Tutored by Teachers, every tutor is an Ontario-certified teacher, sessions run online and in person in Springdale, Brampton, and your first session is free. One-on-one is $40 an hour, small groups of up to 4 are $25 an hour per student, and monthly packages are $300 for one-on-one or $180 for group — 8 sessions each. Start with the free session: you have nothing to lose and a clear picture to gain.

You do not need to wait for a failing grade to get your child help — and you do not need to feel guilty for the signs you missed. What matters is what you do with the signs you see now. Learning gaps compound silently, but they close quickly once the right teacher starts working on them. Trust what you are seeing, and give your child the advantage of being found early.
