What Are EQAO Practice Tests Really Measuring?

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 12, 2026
  • 6 Min

They Measure More Than Right Answers — Here's What Teachers Actually Look For

If you have downloaded an EQAO practice test for your child in Grade 3, Grade 6, or Grade 9, you are not alone. But a practice booklet only helps if you know what it is actually measuring. EQAO is not a test of how much your child has memorized, and a single practice score does not predict the future. Used well, a practice test is one of the clearest windows into how your child reads, writes, and solves problems when nobody is there to help. Here is what teachers look for.

1. What EQAO is really checking overall

EQAO — the Education Quality and Accountability Office — runs Ontario's provincial assessments to check how well students are meeting the Ontario curriculum by the end of a division. Grade 3 covers the primary years, Grade 6 covers the junior years, and Grade 9 assesses mathematics. The next windows are May 4 to June 8, 2027 for Grades 3 and 6, and January 12 to February 5 and June 8 to 30, 2027 for Grade 9 Math. Every question is tied to curriculum expectations your child has already been taught. That is the first reassuring fact: there are no trick topics from the next grade, and no content your child was never supposed to see.

2. Reading: more than finding the answer in the text

Parents often assume reading questions just check whether a child can find information on the page. Some do — those questions ask what the text says directly. But many EQAO reading questions go further. They ask students to infer what a character is feeling and point to evidence, to explain what a word means from context, to identify the main idea, and to connect the text to their own knowledge in a short written response. A child can decode every word fluently and still lose marks if they read quickly, guess, or answer in one vague sentence. When you review a practice test, do not only count right answers. Look at whether your child went back to the text, used specific details, and actually answered the question that was asked.

3. Writing: ideas, organization, and conventions

In the language assessment, students write short responses and longer pieces, such as a news report or a series of paragraphs expressing an opinion. Marking looks at three things together: whether the ideas are developed with relevant details, whether the writing is organized so a reader can follow it, and whether spelling, grammar, and punctuation are controlled enough not to get in the way. This is why a child with wonderful ideas can still score lower than expected — if paragraphs run together, sentences are unfinished, or there is no clear opening and closing, the reader has to work too hard. In a practice test, check for a clear structure first, then for details, then for conventions. That order is usually how improvement happens too.

4. Math: thinking and explaining, not just calculating

EQAO math surprises many families. Alongside multiple-choice questions, students solve problems that require them to show their thinking, choose a strategy, use math language, and explain why an answer makes sense. A correct final number with no work shown may not earn full marks on an open question, while a sound strategy with one small slip can still show strong understanding. Practice tests reveal habits report cards sometimes hide: rushing through multi-step problems, misreading what the question asks, weak mental-math fluency, or never checking an answer. For Grade 9 students especially, the test also measures whether elementary foundations — fractions, ratios, and early algebra — are solid enough for high school math.

5. What a practice score does — and does not — tell you

A practice result is a snapshot of one day, under timed conditions, without a teacher's prompts. It is useful information, not a verdict. It does not measure creativity, effort, or how your child learns over months — and EQAO results for Grades 3 and 6 do not count toward report card marks. What it does tell you is practical: which question types are easy, where your child runs out of time, whether instructions were misread, and which areas need another look. Treat a lower score as a map, not a label. The best question afterwards is not “What did you get?” but “Show me where it got hard.”

6. How to use practice tests at home the right way

Keep it calm and short. Start with one section at a time rather than a full booklet, especially for younger children. Let your child work independently — resist correcting as they go, because you need to see what they can do alone. Afterwards, review together: celebrate good strategies, then pick just two or three errors to talk through. Repeat a similar section a week or two later and look for growth in the process, not only the score. And protect confidence: if practice becomes tears and nightly battles, stop and build the underlying skill first.

7. How tutoring can help

If a practice test shows the same pattern again and again — weak written responses, shaky fractions, or strong knowledge that disappears under time pressure — targeted help can address the exact skill EQAO is measuring. An Ontario-certified teacher can mark practice work the way EQAO scoring guides do, teach a repeatable structure for written answers, and rebuild a math foundation without simply drilling more booklets. An EQAO practice test is best thought of as a rehearsal and a reveal: a rehearsal for the format, timing, and instructions your child will meet on the day, and a reveal of the reading, writing, and thinking habits underneath the marks. You do not need your child to do booklet after booklet. You need one careful look at what the test is really measuring — and then steady, specific help with the few things it shows you. That is how practice turns into confidence.
