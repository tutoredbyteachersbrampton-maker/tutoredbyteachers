How to Help Your Teen Choose Grade 11 and 12 Courses Strategically

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 13, 2026
  • 6 Min

The Prerequisites, Pathways, and Planning Steps That Keep Every Door Open

Course selection at Ontario high schools usually opens in February or March, and for many families with teens in Grade 10 or 11, it feels like the highest-stakes form of the school year. The good news: you do not need to know your teen's future career to choose well. You need a strategy — one that keeps doors open, covers the prerequisites programs actually require, and matches how your teen learns. Here is how to build it together.

1. Start with the ending: what universities actually look at

Ontario university admission is based on Grade 12 marks, not Grade 11: universities calculate an admission average from your teen's top six Grade 12 U or M courses, including the specific prerequisites for the program. Two takeaways: the Grade 12 year counts most, and every program publishes exactly which courses it requires — so there is no guessing. The place to look is eINFO (ontariouniversitiesinfo.ca), where every Ontario university lists program prerequisites. Start every course plan here, with real prerequisites, not hunches.

2. Learn the course codes: U, M, C, O — and why Grade 11 is the real decision

Ontario high school courses in Grades 11 and 12 carry a letter that defines the pathway: U for university, M for university or college, C for college, O for open courses anyone can take, and E for workplace preparation. For university-bound students, only Grade 12 U and M courses count toward admission averages. Grades 9 and 10 are now de-streamed, so streaming only starts to bite in Grade 11 — and that is where families get caught. A student who takes Grade 11 Functions and Applications (MCF3M) instead of Grade 11 Functions (MCR3U) generally cannot jump into Grade 12 Advanced Functions (MHF4U) later, because the M-level course does not satisfy the U-level prerequisite.

3. Work backwards from prerequisites on one page

Pull up the eINFO prerequisites for three or four programs your teen finds interesting and map the chain backwards onto one sheet of paper: Grade 12 courses required, then the Grade 11 courses required to unlock those. The surprises almost always live in math and science — most Ontario engineering programs require ENG4U, Advanced Functions (MHF4U), Calculus and Vectors (MCV4U), Physics (SPH4U), and Chemistry (SCH4U), five specific courses that must be planned two years ahead, while health science programs typically require ENG4U, Biology (SBI4U) and Chemistry (SCH4U), plus Functions. If your teen genuinely does not know what they want, the safest keep-every-door-open bundle is ENG4U, MHF4U, MCV4U, and one or two sciences. One page, worked backwards, beats a binder of brochures.

4. Protect the Grade 11 gateway courses

Grade 11 is where doors quietly close. Grade 11 Functions (MCR3U) is the prerequisite for all three Grade 12 university math courses — Advanced Functions, Calculus and Vectors, and Data Management — and Grade 11 Chemistry (SCH3U), Physics (SPH3U), and Biology (SBI3U) unlock their Grade 12 counterparts. A teen who skips Grade 11 Chemistry cannot simply take Grade 12 Chemistry the next year; the prerequisite has to be made up first. The rule I give families: when in doubt, take the Grade 11 prerequisite and decide in Grade 12 — it is always easier than the reverse. The diploma itself requires 30 credits, 18 compulsory and 12 optional, so there is room for gateway courses.

5. Balance stretch with marks: the honest trade-off

Universities see the number, not the effort behind it, and this is where parents can help most. A well-chosen challenge builds your teen's ability; a brutal course load can cost the admission average. Keep the university-level courses that genuinely serve the plan, and let the remaining spots be courses where your teen can earn strong marks — electives that lift the top-six average are strategic, not cheating. Also factor in the rest of life: a Grade 12 year with four heavy courses plus a part-time job is a recipe for burnout, and burnout tanks marks across the board. Better one strategic swap for a stronger average than a heroic schedule that collapses in February.

6. Don't forget the non-academic requirements

Course planning is not only about marks. The Ontario Secondary School Diploma also requires 40 hours of community involvement and the literacy requirement — and the literacy requirement can derail a plan if it is left too late. The OSSLT is written November 3 to 30, 2026, and again March 30 to April 19, 2027; a teen who has not met the requirement by Grade 11 should plan for the Ontario Secondary School Literacy Course (OLC4O) in Grade 11 or 12 so it does not compete with prerequisite courses in the final year. Build community hours into the summers between grades rather than cramming them into Grade 12. Small planning moves like these keep the final year focused on what counts: the courses that decide admission.

7. How tutoring can help

If your teen is debating whether to stay in a U-level stream, the deciding factor is often confidence in one or two key subjects — most often Grade 11 Functions (MCR3U) or Grade 11 Chemistry (SCH3U), the two gateway courses that unlock the most Grade 12 options. Targeted help with an Ontario-certified teacher can turn a shaky Grade 11 year into a strong foundation for Grade 12, before a weak term closes a stream. Course selection is a plan, not a prophecy. Work backwards from real prerequisites, keep the Grade 11 gateway courses, balance stretch with the average, and leave room for the diploma's other requirements — and the plan will hold even as your teen's interests change. That is the real strategy: one page, honest marks, and no closed doors before Grade 12 even starts.
