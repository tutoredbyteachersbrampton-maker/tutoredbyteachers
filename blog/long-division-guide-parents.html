Long Division Without the Struggle: A Parent's Guide to Helping at Home

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • September 28, 2026
  • 6 Min

It's Not One Skill — It's Four. Here's How to Untangle Them

Few homework topics end a calm evening faster than long division. The page is full of boxes and brackets, your child is staring at it, and you realize you barely remember how you were taught it — let alone how to explain it. Here is the reassuring truth I share with my own students: long division feels hard because it is not one skill. It is four small skills done in a loop. Once you and your child can see the loop, the whole thing gets much less scary.

1. Why long division feels so hard

Addition, subtraction, and multiplication each ask a child to do one kind of thinking at a time. Long division asks for four: dividing, multiplying, subtracting, and then bringing down the next digit — and then the whole cycle repeats. A child who is solid at each of those steps separately can still freeze when the problem demands them in a loop, in order, while keeping every digit in the right column. That is completely normal. In Ontario, students meet long division around Grades 4 and 5 with one-digit divisors, then harder versions — two-digit divisors, decimals — in Grades 5 and 6. Struggling at first is part of the learning curve, not a sign something is wrong.

2. Check the three foundations first

Before blaming the division, check what it is built on. Long division collapses when any of three foundations are shaky: multiplication facts (your child needs them fast, not finger-counted), subtraction with regrouping (borrowing), and place value (understanding what "bringing down" actually means). If your child can do long division perfectly on Monday and not at all on Thursday, the division is probably fine and a tired multiplication fact is the culprit. A quick check: ask for 7 × 8, 12 − 7, and what the 4 means in 432. If any of those are wobbly, that is the place to start — not another page of division.

3. The four-step loop every problem follows

Every long division problem runs the same cycle: Divide — Multiply — Subtract — Bring down. Many classrooms teach a memory sentence for it, like "Does McDonald's Serve Cheese Burgers?" — the first letter of each word matches a step. Take 528 ÷ 4. Divide: 4 goes into 5 once — write 1 above the 5. Multiply: 1 × 4 = 4, under the 5. Subtract: 5 − 4 = 1. Bring down the 2 to make 12 — loop again. 4 goes into 12 three times (3 × 4 = 12, subtract to 0). Bring down the 8. 4 goes into 8 twice, and 8 − 8 = 0. Done: 132. Walk through one problem like this together, saying each step out loud, and your child will have a map for every problem that follows.

4. The three mistakes almost every kid makes

Watching where the errors land tells you exactly what to fix. First, missing zeros in the answer. If the divisor doesn't fit into the working number, a 0 must go in the answer before bringing down — kids skip it, and the whole answer shifts. Second, digits drifting out of column. Long division only works if every digit sits in its place-value column; messy handwriting becomes wrong answers. Third, a wrong answer with no self-check. Teach your child to ask "does this make sense?" — 528 ÷ 4 can't be 1,320, because dividing by 4 makes a number smaller, not bigger. Arithmetic slips fix with practice; missing zeros or misplaced digits mean re-teach that step — more practice of the wrong method only grooves the error deeper.

5. What actually helps at home

Use graph paper. One digit per box keeps columns straight, and for many kids this single change clears up half their errors. Estimate first. Before starting, round the numbers and guess roughly: "528 ÷ 4 is about 500 ÷ 4, so the answer should be around 125." Then the exact answer has something to be checked against. Talk the steps aloud. Have your child narrate "divide, multiply, subtract, bring down" as they go — hearing the loop is how it becomes automatic. One perfect problem beats ten rushed ones. A child who does two problems carefully, checking each step, learns more than one who slogs through a page. Keep sessions short — ten to fifteen minutes of focused practice, then stop while it's going well.

6. When to worry — and when not to

Don't worry if your child is slow but accurate, or if division looks worse in September than it did in June — skills get rusty over summer and sharpen fast with practice. Do pay attention if division is always hard because the foundations underneath it are weak: multiplication facts that never became fluent, or subtraction that still needs regrouping worked out on fingers. Those gaps don't fix themselves, and long division will keep feeling like the problem when it isn't. That is also when helping at home stops working and targeted help from someone who can diagnose the real gap starts to matter.

7. How tutoring can help

An Ontario-certified teacher doesn't just drill more division — they find out where the chain is actually breaking. If it's multiplication facts, we rebuild those. If it's the loop itself, we practise it with the right amount of challenge until it clicks, the way it happens in a classroom but one-on-one or in a small group. A few focused sessions can turn "I hate division" into a skill your child trusts — and with fall progress reports on the way, fixing it now is better than watching it snowball into fractions and algebra later.

Long division looks like a monster until you see it for what it is: a simple four-step loop, repeated until there's nothing left to bring down. Graph paper, a spoken loop, and foundations you can trust will carry most kids through — and for the rest, a little targeted help makes all the difference.
