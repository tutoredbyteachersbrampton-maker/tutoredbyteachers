What Makes a Good Tutor? Red Flags and Green Flags for Brampton Parents

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • November 4, 2026
  • 6 Min

How to Tell, Before You Pay, Whether a Tutor Will Actually Help Your Child

Every parent who starts looking for a tutor in Brampton runs into the same problem: everyone sounds good in an ad. Every service promises better marks, more confidence, and happier homework time. But once the sessions start, the difference between a great tutor and a poor one shows up fast — in your child's attitude, in their homework, and in whether anything actually changes at school. As a teacher, here is what I would look for, and what would make me walk away, before committing to anyone.

1. Green flag: they start by finding out where your child actually is

A good tutor never opens a workbook on day one and starts at page one. They start by asking questions: what is your child finding hard right now, what does their teacher say, what does recent schoolwork look like? Then they check it themselves with a few short tasks. That first check matters because two children in the same grade can be stuck in completely different places — one may need multiplication facts, the other may know the facts but fall apart on word problems. A tutor who skips this step is guessing, and guessing wastes the sessions you are paying for. Ask what that first check looks like and how the tutor will share what they found with you — a good answer is specific, not vague. You should leave that first conversation knowing what your child can already do, what is shaky, and what the tutor plans to tackle first.

2. Green flag: they teach the Ontario curriculum, not just homework answers

There is a big difference between getting tonight's homework finished and actually learning the skill behind it. A strong tutor knows the Ontario curriculum for your child's grade, so the work connects to what is being taught at school and what will be tested next — including, at the right ages, preparation for EQAO in Grades 3 and 6 and the literacy requirement in high school. If a tutor only ever helps with whatever worksheet came home that day, your child may hand in better homework while the underlying gap stays exactly where it was. That is why it helps to ask how sessions connect to school: does the tutor plan around current units, revisit earlier skills when they are weak, and build toward what comes next? Homework help has its place, but it should sit inside a plan, not replace one.

3. Red flag: guaranteed marks, or pressure to sign a long contract

No honest tutor can guarantee an A, a Level 4, or a jump of two grade levels by Christmas. Learning depends on where a child starts, how often they practise, and what is happening at school — and any tutor who promises a specific mark is selling you, not teaching your child. The same caution applies to high-pressure sales: a big upfront package, a contract you cannot leave, or a discount that disappears if you do not decide today. Good tutoring earns the next month by doing good work this month. A free first session, where you can see the teaching before you commit, is a much healthier sign than a hard sell. Also watch for tutors who will not explain their qualifications, who change tutors without telling you, or who charge for materials and assessments that were never mentioned up front. Clear pricing, a named tutor, and a way to pause or stop should all be easy to find before you pay anything.

4. Red flag: the tutor does the work, or your child dreads every session

Watch one session, or at least the first ten minutes of one, and notice who is doing the thinking. If the tutor talks the whole time, writes the answers, and your child nods along, very little learning is happening — your child needs to explain, try, make mistakes, and fix them. That is also why dread matters. A child does not have to love every session, but a good tutor builds a relationship: patient, encouraging, and clear about what success looks like today. If your child consistently cries before sessions, shuts down, or says the tutor is impatient or confusing, believe them. Fit matters as much as qualifications, especially for younger children. A good tutor adjusts when something is not landing — trying a different example, a drawing, or a simpler first step — instead of repeating the same explanation louder. After a session, ask your child one question: could you explain what you learned to someone else? If the answer is yes, the tutor is teaching, not just telling.

5. What progress should look like in the first month

Marks are the slowest thing to move, so do not judge a tutor on the first test alone. In the first few weeks, look for earlier signs: homework takes less fighting, your child can explain one thing they could not explain before, or they attempt problems instead of freezing. A good tutor will also tell you, plainly, what they are working on and what you should see next — and will be honest when something needs more time. If after a month you have heard nothing about what your child is learning, or every report is just "doing great," ask for specifics: which skill, what changed, and what comes next. Vague praise is not a progress report. Keep your own simple notes too: what homework looked like before tutoring, how long it took, and how your child talked about school. Small, steady changes in those everyday signs usually show up weeks before a report card does, and they give you something concrete to discuss with the tutor at the end of the first month.

6. How tutoring can help

At Tutored by Teachers, all of our tutors are Ontario-certified teachers, so the teaching is tied to the curriculum your child meets at school every day. Sessions are one-on-one at CA$40 an hour, or in small groups of no more than four students at CA$25 an hour per student, online or in person in Brampton. We start with a free trial session so you and your child can see the fit before you decide anything — and so we can give you an honest sense of where your child stands and what would help most. If you are comparing tutors, that trial is the simplest test there is: watch how the teacher finds the gap, explains it, and leaves your child feeling more capable than when they arrived. Bring a recent piece of schoolwork to the trial if you can — it gives the teacher something real to work from and gives you something real to judge. A trial that feels rushed, generic, or all talk tells you plenty, and it costs you nothing to walk away from it.

The right tutor is not the one with the flashiest promise. It is the one who finds the real gap, teaches it in a way your child understands, keeps you informed in plain language, and leaves your child a little more confident after every session. Ask the hard questions up front, watch an early session, and trust what you see — your child will tell you, in their attitude and their work, whether you chose well.
