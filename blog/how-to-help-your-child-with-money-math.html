How to Help Your Child With Money Math: Making Change and Budgeting at Home

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • November 16, 2026
  • 6 Min

Real Coins, Real Change, and a Simple Budget Your Child Can Actually Use

Money is the first math most children meet outside of school — at the grocery store, at the corner shop, counting birthday money on the kitchen table. And yet it is the math many kids find hardest to connect with on a worksheet. The numbers are the same as the ones in their math book, but suddenly there are coins to sort, decimals to line up, and a real answer that matters. Here is how to make the most of it.

1. Why money math deserves real practice

Money shows up in the Ontario math curriculum from the early grades right through Grade 8 — counting coins, making change, calculating tax, and eventually building simple budgets. Practising with real money builds the fluency worksheets alone cannot: recognizing coin values at a glance, adding amounts in your head, and checking that change is correct before you walk away.

2. Start with real coins, not worksheets

Before your child can make change, they need to know their coins cold. Empty a jar of loose change onto the table and sort it together: nickels (5 cents), dimes (10 cents), quarters (25 cents), loonies ($1), and toonies ($2). Then practise counting by the coin: count by 5s with nickels, by 10s with dimes, by 25s with quarters. A quick game that works well is "make a dollar" — lay out a pile of coins and challenge your child to find combinations that add to exactly $1.00, then $2.00, then $5.00. Keep it to five minutes and stop while it is still fun. The goal is automatic recognition, so that when a price says $1.85 your child is not still working out what a quarter is worth.

3. Teach making change by counting up

The classic way to make change — subtracting the price from the amount paid — is the way that trips kids up, because it means subtracting decimals under pressure. Shopkeepers use a much friendlier method, and it is the one worth teaching your child: count up from the price to the amount paid. Say the item costs $3.40 and your child pays with a $5 bill. Start at $3.40 and count up: a dime takes you to $3.50, two quarters take you to $4.00, and a loonie takes you to $5.00. The change is a dime, two quarters, and a loonie — $1.60. Practise this at home with play money or real coins, a few times a week, with prices that end in different amounts.

4. The three-jar budget: spend, save, share

Budgeting sounds like a grown-up word, but for a child it can be as simple as three jars on a shelf: one to spend, one to save, and one to share. Whenever your child receives money — allowance, birthday money, tooth-fairy money — help them split it between the three jars and talk about why. The spend jar is for small treats they choose. The save jar is for something bigger they want, and this is where the real learning happens: write the price of the goal on the jar, count what is inside each week, and work out together how many weeks until they can afford it. That is budgeting, subtraction, and delayed gratification in one routine.

5. Turn errands into money-math practice

The grocery store is the best money-math classroom there is, and it costs nothing extra. Give your child a small job on each trip: a $20 budget for three items on the list, and the task of keeping a running total as you shop. Ask them to estimate before you reach the till — "about how much do you think this will be?" — and then compare the estimate to the real total. Comparing prices is another easy win: two boxes of cereal, one $4.99 and one $6.49 — which is cheaper, and by how much? Older kids can work out the unit price: which bag of apples is the better deal per kilogram?

6. When your child keeps struggling

If money math stays hard even with practice, the problem is usually underneath the money, not in it. The most common sticking points are place value (a dime is 10 cents, not 1 cent, and $1.25 is not the same as $1.52), counting by 25s, and lining up decimals when adding prices like $2.49 + $1.75. The fix is to go back to the concrete: lay out the coins, count them out loud, and write the amounts down with the decimal points lined up in a column. Keep the numbers small until the method is solid, and resist the urge to jump straight to a calculator — the calculator hides exactly the thinking your child needs to practise.

7. How tutoring can help

When money struggles point to a deeper gap — place value, decimals, or multi-digit subtraction — a few focused sessions with an Ontario-certified teacher can close it quickly. A teacher can spot exactly where the breakdown happens, whether it is coin values, decimal alignment, or mental-math strategies, and build the missing skill with the same kind of hands-on practice described here. If your child's fall progress report flagged number sense or problem solving, money math is one of the most encouraging places to start rebuilding — the wins are visible, and your child can feel them.

Money math is not a separate subject — it is the math your child already knows, applied to something they care about. A jar of coins, a grocery list, and a few minutes a week are all it takes to turn an abstract worksheet skill into something your child can actually use. Start small, keep it real, and let your child be the one holding the money.
