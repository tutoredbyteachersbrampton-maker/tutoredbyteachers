How to Help Your Child With Long Division Word Problems at Home

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • November 26, 2026
  • 6 Min

The Words Are the Hard Part — Not the Division

Here is a scene many Brampton parents know well: your child can do 96 ÷ 8 on a worksheet without a problem, but hand them the same numbers wrapped in a story — "96 cookies shared equally among 8 friends, how many does each friend get?" — and suddenly they freeze. The division is not the problem. The words are. Long division word problems ask children to do two jobs at once: understand the story, then do the math. Once you know how to split those two jobs apart, helping at home becomes much easier. Here is the method I teach my own students.

1. Why word problems feel harder than plain division

A bare division question gives your child everything: the two numbers and the operation, right there on the page. A word problem hides all of that inside a story. Your child has to figure out which numbers matter, which operation to use, and what the answer actually means — and only then do the division. That is three thinking steps before the math even starts, which is why a child who is capable at the long division steps can stare at a word problem and say, "I don't get it." The good news: the reading part is a skill, and it can be practised separately from the dividing part.

2. The 4-step method: read, picture it, divide, check

Teach your child to run every word problem through four steps, in order. First, read the problem twice — once to get the story, once to find the numbers. Second, picture it: ask, "What is being shared, and among how many?" If they can draw it or act it out with objects on the table, even better. Third, write the division and solve it, step by step. Fourth, check the answer against the story: does the number make sense, and does it answer the question that was actually asked? When a child works the steps in order, the problem stops feeling like one big wall and becomes four small, doable moves.

3. Worked example: the cookies

Try this one together: "A baker makes 96 cookies and packs them equally into 8 boxes. How many cookies are in each box?" Read it twice. Picture it: 96 cookies being shared into 8 equal groups. The division is 96 ÷ 8. Work it out: 8 goes into 9 once (8 × 1 = 8), leaving 1; bring down the 6 to make 16; 8 goes into 16 exactly twice (8 × 2 = 16). So the answer is 12 cookies per box. Now check it: 12 × 8 = 96, which matches the cookies we started with. That final check — multiplying the answer back — is the habit that catches most careless errors before they reach the teacher.

4. The remainder question: what do we do with what's left over?

Remainders are where word problems get tricky, because the right thing to do with a remainder depends on the story. Take this one: "135 pencils are packed into boxes of 9. How many boxes are needed?" The division is 135 ÷ 9 = 15 exactly, no remainder — 15 boxes. But change the numbers: "140 pencils are packed into boxes of 9." Now 140 ÷ 9 = 15 remainder 5, and those 5 leftover pencils still need a box — so the answer is 16 boxes, not 15. In another story, the remainder might be thrown away ("how many full boxes?") or become the answer itself ("how many pencils are left over?"). Teach your child to always re-read the question after dividing and ask: "What is it actually asking for?" That one habit prevents most remainder mistakes.

5. The three mistakes I see most often

First, dividing the numbers in the wrong order — the big number being shared goes inside the division bracket, and the number of groups goes outside. Second, forgetting the units: an answer of "12" means nothing until your child can say "12 cookies per box." Third, skipping the check: a quick multiplication of the answer by the divisor catches most errors in seconds, but children almost never do it unless it becomes a habit. When you mark homework together, do not just circle the wrong answer — ask which of these three happened. Naming the mistake stops it repeating.

6. Ten minutes a day is enough — use real life

You do not need a workbook to practise word problems. Real life is full of them. Splitting a restaurant bill, sharing a bag of apples equally, figuring out how many weeks of allowance it takes to save for a toy — these are division word problems in disguise, and they teach the reading skill better than a worksheet because the story is real. Ask your child to invent one word problem for you each day, too — making up the problem teaches the same thinking the test demands. With fall progress reports now in parents' hands and the Grade 3 and 6 EQAO tests coming May 4 to June 8, 2027, a few minutes of this kind of practice each week builds real confidence before test season arrives.

7. How tutoring can help

If your child's homework keeps coming home with word problems left blank — or finished but wrong — a few focused sessions with an Ontario-certified teacher can close the gap quickly. A tutor can spot whether the problem is the reading, the division steps, or the remainder, and then practise exactly that piece until it clicks. Working one-on-one, or in a small group of no more than four students, your child gets the patient, step-by-step coaching that a busy classroom cannot always provide. And because the first session is free, you can see whether it helps before committing to anything.

Long division word problems look hard because they ask for two skills at once — understanding the story and doing the division. Teach your child to slow down, read twice, picture the sharing, divide carefully, and always check the answer against the story. Practise a little each week with real-life sharing, and the problems that used to cause tears will start to feel routine. And if the gap is bigger than a few minutes at the kitchen table can fix, that is exactly what a good tutor is for.
