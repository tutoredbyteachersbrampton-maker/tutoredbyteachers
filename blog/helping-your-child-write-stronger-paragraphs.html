Helping Your Child Write Stronger Paragraphs: The Hamburger Method and Beyond

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 21, 2026
  • 6 Min

One Simple Structure That Turns Rambling Sentences Into Real Writing

If you have ever read your child's paragraph and thought, "I can see the ideas, but they are all jumbled together," you are not alone. Most students in Grades 3 to 8 do not struggle with ideas — they struggle with structure. They know what they want to say; they just do not know how to organize it. That is where the hamburger method comes in. It is a simple, visual way of teaching paragraph structure that Ontario teachers have used for years, and it is one of the easiest writing tools you can practise at home together.

1. What the hamburger method actually is

The idea is simple: a good paragraph is built like a hamburger. The top bun is the topic sentence — the sentence that tells the reader what the paragraph is about. The fillings (the lettuce, cheese, and patty) are the supporting details — the reasons, examples, and explanations that back up the topic sentence. The bottom bun is the concluding sentence — a wrap-up that restates the main point. When your child can "see" the paragraph this way, the writing task stops feeling like a blank page and starts feeling like a recipe. You can even draw the hamburger together before they write: label the top bun, fill in three or four detail lines, then write the closing sentence. That one drawing does more work than ten reminders to "write a better paragraph."

2. The topic sentence test every parent can use

The most common paragraph problem is a missing or muddy topic sentence. Here is a quick test: read the first sentence, then cover the rest of the paragraph. Can you predict what details should follow? If yes, the topic sentence is doing its job. If no, the paragraph probably drifts. Coach your child to write a topic sentence that makes one clear claim, such as "Recess should be longer at our school" instead of "Recess is a thing I will talk about." A specific claim gives every following sentence a job: prove this. If a detail does not prove it, the detail does not belong in that paragraph.

3. Help them find real supporting details

The "filling" is where most paragraphs fall apart. Kids either give one vague detail ("it is good") or list facts that are not really connected to the topic sentence. The fix is asking the right question: "What makes you say that?" or "Give me an example." A child who says dogs make the best pets can usually say why when you ask — "my neighbour's dog always comes to greet me" is a concrete example that beats a paragraph of "dogs are great." In Ontario classrooms, writing rubrics reward specific, developed details, not just lots of words. Practise this at the dinner table: make any claim, and ask your child to defend it with three examples. It is the same muscle, and it is fun when it is about pizza toppings.

4. Transitions: the glue that holds it together

Even strong details feel choppy without transition words — "first," "for example," "in addition," "also," "because," "finally." These little words tell the reader how the details connect, and teachers notice when they are missing. Keep a short list on the fridge. When your child writes, ask them to start at least two of their detail sentences with a different transition word. It is a small habit, and it is one of the fastest ways to make writing sound organized. Over time they will use these words without thinking — which is exactly what strong writers do.

5. The concluding sentence is not decoration

Many kids simply stop writing when they run out of things to say — no closing sentence at all. Teach them the bottom bun: one sentence that restates the topic sentence in different words. Not a repeat, a restatement. "That is why longer recess would make school better for everyone" echoes the opening without copying it. This single habit lifts a paragraph from "done" to "finished," and it is exactly the skill that later becomes the conclusion of essays in high school. If your child writes a paragraph without one, hand it back and ask, "Where is the bottom bun?" — the metaphor will click faster than any lecture.

6. Beyond the hamburger: going deeper

Once the hamburger structure is comfortable, many Ontario teachers introduce a step further — making sure each detail is not just stated but explained. A useful follow-up prompt is "So what?" After every detail, your child asks, "So what does this prove?" That question turns a list of facts into actual reasoning. Another next step is comparing: can your child write a paragraph that gives two sides of an idea, or describes something using an example and a non-example? The hamburger gets them writing organized paragraphs; "so what?" gets them writing thoughtful ones. Do not rush this stage — a confident hamburger writer is already ahead of most of the class.

7. How tutoring can help

If your child's writing comes home with comments like "add more detail" or "organize your ideas," one-on-one coaching can fix the pattern quickly. An Ontario-certified teacher can show your child exactly where their paragraphs break down — topic sentence, details, or closing — and practise the fix with them in real time, the way a classroom of thirty never quite allows. Writing confidence builds fast once the structure clicks, and it carries over into every subject: science explanations, history answers, even the OSSLT in Grade 10. With fall progress reports on the horizon, a few sessions now can make a visible difference.

Strong paragraphs are not a talent — they are a structure anyone can learn. The hamburger method gives your child the blueprint: a clear topic sentence, real supporting details, and a closing that ties it together. Practise it a few times at the kitchen table, and watch their writing change from a jumble of sentences into paragraphs that actually convince a reader.
