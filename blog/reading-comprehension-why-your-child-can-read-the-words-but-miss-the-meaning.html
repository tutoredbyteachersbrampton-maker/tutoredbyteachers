Reading Comprehension: Why Your Child Can Read the Words but Miss the Meaning

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • November 22, 2026
  • 6 Min

Decoding Is Only Half the Job — Here's What Understanding Really Takes

Your child reads a page out loud — every word correct, not a stumble in sight. Then you ask, "So what happened?" and get a blank stare. If that feels familiar, you are not imagining a problem, and your child is not being lazy. Reading the words and understanding them are two different skills, and a child can be strong at one while struggling with the other. The good news: it is one of the most fixable reading problems there is.

1. Reading has two jobs, not one

Think of reading as two jobs at once. The first is decoding: turning letters into sounds and sounds into words. The second is comprehension: building meaning from those words — picturing what is happening and connecting it to what you already know. But for some children, decoding stays effortful, or the comprehension habit never quite switches on — and the result is a child who can read a whole page fluently and still not know what it said. Ontario reading assessments are built to catch exactly this: EQAO questions ask not just what the text said, but what it means.

2. Why the meaning slips away

There is usually a reason, and it is rarely a lack of intelligence. The most common one is vocabulary: a child can sound out "reluctant" without knowing what it means, and a few unknown words per paragraph are enough to blur the whole passage. Another is background knowledge — a story about a world your child has never encountered is harder to follow. Some children read too fast, racing to the bottom of the page as if finishing were the point. Others read word by word, so slowly that by the end of a sentence they have forgotten how it started. None of these mean your child cannot learn to understand what they read — they mean understanding needs to be taught directly, the same way sounding out words was.

3. The signs to watch for at home

You do not need a test to spot this. The clearest sign is the one you have probably already seen: your child can read a passage aloud but cannot retell it in their own words, even right after finishing. Other signs: going blank on "why" and "how" questions; choosing easier books than they can decode; avoiding chapter books; and saying they "hate reading" — which often means reading feels pointless, because nothing is landing. If two or three of these sound familiar, comprehension — not decoding — is the piece to work on. With fall progress reports coming home around now, check the reading comments, not just the level: teachers often note whether a student is "making connections" or "retelling with detail."

4. The before, during, and after routine

The most effective habit is also the simplest: stop treating reading as one long, uninterrupted activity. Before your child starts, spend a minute on the title, the pictures, and the first paragraph, and ask: "What do you think this is going to be about?" That prediction gives their brain something to hold onto. During reading, pause every page or two and ask them to tell you what just happened in one sentence — not a quiz, just a check-in. If they cannot, go back and re-read that part slowly together. After reading, ask for a retell: "Tell me the story like you are telling a friend who has never read it." Help at first with prompts — "Who was in it? What was the problem? How did it end?" Soon, most children do this on their own. Keep sessions to about 15 minutes, daily, rather than one long weekend push.

5. Ask questions that build understanding

The questions you ask after reading matter as much as the reading itself — and the best ones have no single right answer. Instead of "Did you like it?", try "Why do you think she did that?" or "What do you think will happen next, and why?" These force your child to go back into the text, find evidence, and think — precisely what Ontario reading assessments measure. When your child answers, follow up with the most powerful question a teacher has: "How do you know?" It teaches them to point back to the words on the page instead of guessing. And when they hit a word they do not know, do not just give them the definition. Ask them to guess from the sentence around it first.

6. When reading aloud helps — and when it hides the problem

Reading aloud is a wonderful tool, but it can also hide a comprehension problem. When your child reads aloud to you, you hear the decoding — and if that sounds fine, it is easy to assume the understanding is fine too. So mix it up. Sometimes, have your child read silently and then tell you about it; silent reading is where comprehension actually lives, and it is how most school reading works from Grade 4 onward. And when your child is the one reading, do not correct every small stumble: stop only for errors that change the meaning of the sentence.

7. How tutoring can help

If the retell-and-question routine helps a little but the gap is still there, targeted support can make a real difference. An Ontario-certified teacher can pinpoint whether the issue is vocabulary, background knowledge, fluency, or the habit of reading passively — and then work on exactly that, with texts at the right level and questions that build the skill step by step. Because comprehension work is mostly conversation — discussing, predicting, connecting, retelling — it responds well to one-on-one sessions and small groups of up to four students, where your child gets far more chances to talk about what they read than in a full classroom. With Grade 3 and 6 EQAO reading tests coming May 4 to June 8, 2027, starting now means the skill is solid well before test season.

A child who reads the words but misses the meaning is not behind in some permanent way — they are missing one specific skill, and that skill can be taught. Start with the retell, ask "how do you know," and keep daily reading short and conversational. Within a few weeks, you will hear the difference.
