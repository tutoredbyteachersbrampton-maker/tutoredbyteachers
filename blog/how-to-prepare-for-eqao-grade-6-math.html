How to Prepare for EQAO Grade 6 Math: A Parent's Month-by-Month Plan

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • November 23, 2026
  • 6 Min

Six Months of Small Steps Beats Six Weeks of Panic

The Grade 6 EQAO math test is written in the spring — this year the window for Grades 3 and 6 runs May 4 to June 8, 2027 — and most families do not start thinking about it until April. That is exactly when the stress starts. The truth is that EQAO preparation is not a sprint; it is a slow, steady build. A little bit of focused practice each month, starting now, does far more than a frantic few weeks of worksheets in May. Here is a realistic month-by-month plan you can follow from November through test week.

1. What the Grade 6 EQAO math test actually covers

The test measures the math your child is learning in Grade 6 under the Ontario curriculum: number sense and operations (including fractions, decimals, and percents), algebra and patterning, data management, spatial sense (geometry and measurement), and financial literacy. Questions are a mix of multiple choice and open response, and many are set in real-life situations — reading a graph, figuring out a price, or finding a pattern. It is not a test of tricks. The students who do well are the ones who understand the ideas and can explain their thinking, not the ones who memorized the most procedures.

2. November–December: Find the gaps while there's time to fix them

Your child's fall progress report has just come home, and it is your best starting point. Look past the level and read the teacher's comments: is your child "progressing with difficulty" in a particular strand, or does the comment mention rushing, missing steps, or trouble explaining answers? Those are your gaps. Ask the teacher one direct question at the parent-teacher interview: "What is the one math skill my child most needs to strengthen before spring?" Then spend 10 to 15 minutes, three times a week, on that one thing — multiplication facts, fraction basics, or multi-step word problems. One gap at a time, before the new year, is the whole strategy for these two months.

3. January–February: Build the foundations

The winter term is when Grade 6 math gets harder, and it is the best time to lock in the foundations the test leans on most. Three skills matter more than anything else: fluency with multiplication and division facts, comfort with fractions and decimals (including switching between them), and reading a problem carefully before solving it. A simple routine works: five minutes of fact practice, then two or three real problems from the current unit. Use everyday math, too — splitting a restaurant bill, measuring ingredients while doubling a recipe, or estimating the total at the grocery store. When math shows up in real life, it stops feeling like a school subject and starts feeling like a tool.

4. March: Word problems and showing your thinking

By March, shift the focus to word problems and open-response questions — the part of EQAO where capable students lose the most marks. The pattern is almost always the same: a student knows the math but rushes, skips a step, or writes only the answer. Teach your child a simple habit: underline what the question is asking, circle the numbers that matter, solve, then check whether the answer makes sense. For open responses, the answer alone is not enough — the explanation earns marks. Have your child say the solution out loud first, in full sentences, and then write it down. If they can explain it to you, they can explain it on the test.

5. April: Practise under test conditions

April is the month for EQAO practice materials, which EQAO publishes on its website. Work through a sample booklet together, but do it properly: a quiet room, a timer, and no help during the session. Afterwards, go through the mistakes together — not to scold, but to sort them into two piles: "didn't know it" and "knew it but slipped." The second pile is usually bigger, and it is the easiest to fix: misreading, arithmetic slips, and unfinished questions. One timed practice a week through April, plus a short review of the errors, is enough. Resist the urge to drill every night; tired kids make more mistakes, not fewer.

6. May: The final weeks — calm, confident, ready

In the weeks before the test window opens, the goal changes from learning to confidence. Keep practice light and familiar: review the trickiest problems from April, revisit the facts that were shaky, and stop introducing anything new. The night before each test day, the plan is simple — a normal dinner, a normal bedtime, and a real breakfast in the morning. Remind your child of two things: the test shows what they have learned, not who they are, and a question they cannot do is just one question. Tell them to skip it, answer everything else, and come back if there is time. A calm child with a plan always outperforms a nervous child with a crammed brain.

7. How tutoring can help

If the fall progress report flagged a real gap — fractions that never clicked, word problems that always go sideways — a few months of targeted help can change the whole spring. An Ontario-certified teacher can find the exact missing piece, rebuild it step by step, and work through EQAO-style questions the way the test actually asks them. Starting in November or January gives a tutor time to fix the foundation instead of just patching over it in April. Our one-on-one sessions are $40 an hour, and small groups of up to four students are $25 an hour per student, online or in person in Brampton — and the first session is free, so you can see whether it fits before committing to anything.

The Grade 6 EQAO math test is months away, and that is good news — it means you have time to prepare properly. A little bit each month, a clear picture of the gaps, and a calm test week will do more for your child than any last-minute cram session. Start with the progress report, pick one thing to fix, and build from there.
