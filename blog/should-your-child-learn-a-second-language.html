Should Your Child Learn a Second Language? The Case for French and Beyond

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • November 24, 2026
  • 6 Min

French, Spanish, Punjabi, Mandarin — Here's What a Second Language Really Gives Your Child

If you live in Brampton, your child is already growing up in one of the most multilingual cities in Canada. On any street here you will hear Punjabi, Hindi, Urdu, Gujarati, Spanish, Tagalog, and French — often in the same schoolyard. So when parents ask me whether their child should learn a second language, my honest answer is: they are already surrounded by the opportunity. The real question is whether to make it deliberate. Here is what a second language actually gives your child, how the options work in Ontario schools, and how to start — at any age, without turning your evenings into a battleground.

1. The benefits are real — and they go beyond the language itself

Children who learn a second language get more than a new way to say hello. Switching between two languages exercises the brain's attention and self-control systems: a bilingual child is constantly choosing which language to use and holding the other one back, and that daily practice strengthens focus and flexible thinking. It also builds a deeper understanding of how language works in general, which shows up in stronger first-language reading and writing. And in a country with two official languages, French opens real doors later on — federal government jobs, many Ontario public-service roles, and post-secondary programs all value bilingualism. Even a few years of steady exposure builds skills that carry into every subject.

2. French is the obvious first choice in Ontario — here's why

In Ontario, French is not just another subject: it is part of the curriculum. Every student in a publicly funded school takes Core French starting in Grade 4, and it continues through Grade 9. For families who want more, school boards offer French Immersion — where much of the school day is taught in French — and Extended French programs that sit between Core and Immersion. In Peel, French Immersion typically starts in Grade 1, and that is worth knowing early, because the entry point comes before most parents are thinking about it. If French Immersion interests you, look into it while your child is in kindergarten — the application windows come around fast, and missing one can mean waiting a year.

3. But the "best" second language is the one your family will actually use

French is the practical school choice, but it is not the only good one. If grandparents speak Punjabi, a heritage language keeps your child connected to family in a way no classroom can replace — and research consistently shows that a strong first or home language supports, rather than holds back, learning English and French at school. Spanish, Mandarin, and other widely spoken languages are equally valid if your family has a reason to use them: relatives, travel, community, or your child's own interest. The language that gets spoken at the dinner table, in video calls with grandparents, or in songs your child loves will always beat the one that only exists in a workbook. Follow the connection, not the trend.

4. It's never too late — and it's never too early, either

Younger children do pick up pronunciation and natural-sounding speech more easily, which is one reason French Immersion starts in Grade 1. But older children have advantages too: they understand grammar, they can read, and they can learn deliberately. A Grade 7 student starting French in Core French can absolutely catch up with focused practice, and a teenager who decides to learn Spanish because of a trip or a friend can make remarkable progress in a year. The myth to ignore is that there is a single window that closes. Consistency — a little bit, often — matters far more than starting age.

5. How to build a second language at home without pressure

The families who succeed at this almost never run a formal lesson at the kitchen table. They weave the language into things the child already enjoys: a cartoon in French on Saturday morning, a simple song in the car, labelling a few things around the house, a short video call with a relative who speaks the language. Fifteen minutes a day of something fun will do more than an hour of forced drilling once a week. If your child is in Core French or French Immersion, ask them to teach you one new word a day — teaching is the best way to remember, and it makes the language something they own rather than something done to them.

6. What if your child is struggling in French at school?

French is the subject I hear about most from parents whose child is otherwise doing fine. The usual pattern: the class moves fast, the vocabulary piles up, and by the time the report card arrives in the fall progress report or the February term report, your child has quietly fallen behind. The fix is almost never "more homework." It is usually a gap in the basics — verb patterns, common vocabulary, or simply not hearing enough French outside class. Short, regular practice with a teacher who can explain things in English first and then build the French on top of it closes those gaps quickly. And if French Immersion is genuinely not the right fit, that is a real conversation to have with the teacher, not a failure — some children thrive in Immersion and some do far better in Core French with strong support.

7. How tutoring can help

If your child is falling behind in French — or in English reading and writing alongside it — targeted help can make the difference between dreading the subject and enjoying it. An Ontario-certified teacher can pinpoint exactly where the gap is, rebuild the missing vocabulary and patterns, and give your child the confidence to speak up in class instead of staying quiet. A few focused sessions are often enough to turn French from the subject your child avoids into one they can handle — and for students in French Immersion, that support can be what keeps the program working for them.

A second language is one of the few gifts you can give your child that grows with them for life — in school, in work, and in the family connections it keeps alive. Whether it is French through school, a heritage language at home, or a language your child chose for themselves, the best time to start is now, and the best way is a little bit, often, with something they enjoy. In a city as multilingual as Brampton, your child is already halfway there.
