Online vs In-Person Tutoring: Which Is Right for Your Child?

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • September 23, 2026
  • 6 Min

An Honest Teacher's Take on Both Formats — and How to Choose

Almost every parent who calls us asks some version of the same question: "Is online tutoring as effective as in-person?" And underneath that question is the real worry — will my kid actually focus on a screen? It is a fair question. As an Ontario-certified teacher who has taught both ways, here is my honest answer: both formats work, but they work for different kids and different reasons. Let me walk you through how to think about it so you can choose with confidence instead of guessing.

1. The real question isn't "which is better?" — it's "which fits my child?"

Parents often come to me hoping there is a single right answer. There isn't. The format matters less than the fit between the format and your child. A focused Grade 8 student who is comfortable on a laptop can make huge progress online, while a fidgety Grade 3 student who needs to move and touch things will get far more out of sitting at a real table with a real teacher. The same tutor, the same curriculum, the same price — and a completely different outcome, because the child is different. So instead of asking which format wins in general, ask which format fits the kid sitting in front of you.

2. Which students thrive with online tutoring

Older students — roughly Grades 6 and up — tend to do very well online. They are used to working on screens at school, they can manage a keyboard and a webcam, and they don't need a teacher physically beside them to stay on task. Students who are shy in person sometimes open up more on a screen, where the pressure of sitting across from an adult feels lower. Online also suits busy families: no driving across Brampton at 5pm in February, no rearranging schedules around pickup and drop-off. If your child is self-directed enough to sit down, open a laptop, and stay present for an hour, online tutoring removes the biggest barrier most families face — logistics.

3. Which students do better in person

Younger kids, roughly kindergarten through Grade 5, usually do better in person — and I'll tell you why honestly. At that age, attention follows the teacher's physical presence. A real person across the table can redirect a wandering mind in a second, hand over manipulatives for math, or watch exactly how your child forms letters. Kids who struggle with focus, who are anxious, or who learn best by doing rather than watching also tend to make faster progress face to face. If your child disappears into YouTube the moment a screen turns on, in-person sessions take that temptation off the table entirely.

4. What makes online tutoring actually effective

Here is where I get blunt: bad online tutoring is very bad. If the "tutor" is a university student reading from a script while your child stares at a shared screen, nobody learns anything. Good online tutoring has a few non-negotiables. The camera stays on — for the student and the teacher. There is an interactive whiteboard both sides can write on, not just a teacher talking at a slide deck. And crucially, it is the same teacher every session, building the relationship week after week. That's how we run our online sessions: Ontario-certified teachers, live whiteboard work, and your child's work saved and reviewed between sessions. When those pieces are in place, online math and English sessions are every bit as productive as in-person ones.

5. What makes in-person tutoring better

In person has advantages a screen simply can't copy. Hands-on materials — counting blocks, fraction tiles, printed worksheets your child can mark up — matter a lot for younger learners and for concrete math topics. The teacher can see everything: the eraser marks, the half-finished work, the moment confusion crosses your child's face. And for many families, getting out of the house is a feature, not a bug. If home is full of siblings, noise, and distractions, a quiet session at our Springdale location gives your child something rare — one focused hour with a teacher and nothing else competing for attention. That focused environment is often the whole point.

6. Does the subject matter? Math and English both work online

This is the second question parents ask me, and the answer is straightforward: with the right teacher and the right tools, both math and English work online. Math is actually a natural fit — the whole session happens on a shared whiteboard where the teacher watches each step of the solution as your child writes it. English works too: reading passages, writing practice, and essay feedback are all screen-friendly. What matters far more than the subject is the teacher's familiarity with the Ontario curriculum — knowing exactly what a Grade 7 math unit or a Grade 10 English essay expects, and teaching to that standard. A certified teacher with the curriculum in their bones delivers quality in either format; an uncertified tutor delivers weak sessions in both.

7. A straightforward cost comparison

Here's something parents find surprising: at Tutored by Teachers, the format doesn't change the price — the session type does. One-on-one tutoring is $40 per hour, whether it's online or in person at our Springdale, Brampton location. Small group sessions (maximum 4 students) are $25 per hour per student in either format. We also offer monthly packages: $300 per month for 8 one-on-one sessions, or $180 per month for 8 group sessions. The real cost difference between formats isn't our fee — it's everything around it. In-person costs you driving time and gas; online costs you nothing extra. Be honest about which of those matters more to your family's weekly schedule.

8. The honest way to decide: don't guess — try it

You don't have to choose blindly. The only reliable way to know which format fits your child is to watch them in it. Every family that comes to us gets a free first session, and you can book it online or in person — whichever you're leaning toward. Pay attention during that session: Is your child engaged or drifting? Are they comfortable, or fidgeting? Do they leave energized or drained? One hour tells you more than any article can. And if you try online and it isn't clicking, switching to in-person is always an option — the teacher and the plan stay the same, only the room changes.

The bottom line is simpler than most parents expect: the format doesn't do the teaching — the teacher does. Pick the format that fits your child's age, attention, and your family's schedule, make sure the tutor is a certified Ontario teacher using the right tools, and you'll get results either way.
