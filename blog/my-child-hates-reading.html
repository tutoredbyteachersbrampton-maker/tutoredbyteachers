My Child Hates Reading: 7 Ways to Turn Reluctant Readers Around

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • September 29, 2026
  • 6 Min

Why Kids Push Books Away — and the Practical Fixes That Actually Work

Almost every parent of a reluctant reader has the same story: books arrive home from the library and sit untouched, reading logs become a nightly battle, and "just 20 minutes" feels like a punishment. As a teacher, I want you to hear this first: a child who hates reading is rarely lazy and rarely broken. Usually, reading just doesn't feel good for them yet — because it is hard, or boring, or wrapped in too much pressure. Once you figure out which of those is the real problem, you can fix it. Here are seven strategies that work in real classrooms and real living rooms.

1. Find out whether it's "won't" or "can't"

Before anything else, separate attitude from ability. A child who avoids reading because the words are genuinely hard — slow decoding, losing their place, guessing instead of reading — is not being stubborn; they are protecting themselves from something that feels bad. Listen to your child read a paragraph aloud from a book at their grade level. If they stumble on many words, the books are too hard and every session feels like work. Ask their teacher whether the reading level is matched properly, and for young readers check whether daily fluency practice is in place. A child who simply won't choose to read but reads fine when they do has a motivation problem, which is easier to solve — the rest of this list is for them.

2. Let them read what they actually want

The biggest turn-off for reluctant readers is being handed books they never chose. Comics and graphic novels, joke books, sports biographies, books about video games, how-to and hobby guides, magazines, even well-written nonfiction about animals or machines — it all counts as reading. The Ontario curriculum itself treats graphic texts and real-world materials as legitimate reading, so don't dismiss them. Take a trip to the Brampton library and let your child pick anything, with one rule: they don't have to finish it. A child who abandons three books and loves the fourth is reading more than a child forced through one miserable chapter book.

3. Lower the bar to almost nothing

For a child who dreads reading, twenty minutes feels like a mountain. Start with ten minutes, or even five — an amount so small they can't fail at it. Attach it to something pleasant: reading time on the couch with a blanket, reading aloud to a pet, or a chapter while dinner cooks. End each session on a good note, ideally in the middle of something interesting, so tomorrow feels like coming back to something rather than facing a chore. As the habit settles, stretch the time naturally. A short daily routine that sticks beats an ambitious one that collapses in a week.

4. Model it — let them catch you reading

Children copy what they see far more than what they are told. If the house has screens everywhere and no adult ever sits down with a book, "reading is important" sounds like a slogan. You don't need to read novels — a newspaper, a cookbook, a magazine, a sports site on a tablet all count. Some families do a quiet reading block where everyone, parents included, reads their own thing for twenty minutes. When reading is just what the family does, the message lands without a lecture.

5. Make it social, not solitary

Many reluctant readers imagine reading as sitting alone with something boring. Break that picture: read the first chapter aloud together and leave the cliffhanger for them, take turns reading a page each, read the same book so you can talk about it like a little book club, or listen to an audiobook in the car and follow along in the physical book. For younger kids, reading to a sibling or even a stuffed animal lowers the stakes — nobody is judging. Reading becomes something shared instead of something assigned.

6. Connect reading to school wins they can feel

Sometimes kids resist reading because they don't see the point. You can connect the dots gently: show them that the science unit, the word problems in math, and the research for a project all go faster when reading feels easy. If your child is in Grade 3 or 6, the EQAO reading test this spring (May 4 to June 8, 2027) is a real, concrete reason reading fluency matters — and if they are approaching Grade 10, the OSSLT reading section in the fall window (November 3 to 30, 2026) is even closer. A near-term goal turns reading practice into preparation with a payoff they can picture.

7. How tutoring can help

Some reluctant readers need more than a better routine. If your child is behind their grade's reading level, avoids reading aloud, or freezes when asked questions about what they read, an Ontario-certified teacher can find exactly where the gap is — decoding, fluency, vocabulary, or comprehension — and work on just that. One-on-one sessions remove the embarrassment of reading in front of classmates, and a teacher knows how to pick texts at the right level so reading starts feeling successful again. Often a few focused weeks are enough to change how a child feels about books, and the free first session is a good no-risk way to see where your child stands.

No child is born hating reading — somewhere along the way it stopped feeling good, and your job is simply to help it feel good again. Give them the right level, the right books, a tiny daily habit, and a house where reading is normal, and most reluctant readers come around. The earlier you start, the easier the turn — so if reading has been a battle in your house, this fall is a good time to change the script.
