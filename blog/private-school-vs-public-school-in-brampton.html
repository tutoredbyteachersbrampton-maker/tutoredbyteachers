Private School vs. Public School in Brampton: A Parent's Comparison

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 31, 2026
  • 6 Min

Cost, Class Size, Programs, and Fit — What Actually Differs, and How to Choose

Every fall in Brampton, the same conversation starts in kitchens and school parking lots: should we look at private school? It often starts with a progress report that raises questions, a class that feels too big, or a neighbour whose child seems to be thriving somewhere else. Private school can be a wonderful fit for some families — and completely unnecessary for others. The honest answer is that neither system is automatically better. What matters is the fit between your child, your family, and the school itself. Here is a clear, teacher's-eye comparison of what actually differs between private and public school in Brampton, and how to decide.

1. The first difference: who pays, and what that buys

Public schools in Ontario are funded by the province, so there is no tuition for families who live in the school's boundary. In Brampton, that means the Peel District School Board and the Dufferin-Peel Catholic District School Board, plus French-language boards for families who qualify. Private schools are funded mainly by tuition paid by families, and that tuition varies enormously from school to school — and on top of it often come uniforms, meals, trips, and activity fees. Before you fall in love with any private school, get the full yearly cost in writing, not just the headline tuition figure. Then ask the harder question: what does that money buy that your child would not get otherwise? Sometimes the answer is real — smaller classes, a specialist program, a faith-based environment. Sometimes the honest answer is a shorter commute and nicer facilities, which may matter less to your child's learning than you think.

2. Class size and individual attention

Smaller classes are the most common reason parents consider private school, and it is a fair instinct: a teacher with fewer students can give each child more feedback and notice struggles sooner. Private schools in Brampton do tend to have smaller classes than public schools, but class size varies a lot within both systems, and small is not automatically better. A small class with weak teaching helps no one, and a strong public-school teacher with a full class still knows their students well. When you tour any school, ask how teachers track individual progress, how often you will hear from them, and what happens when a child falls behind in reading or math. The answers to those questions tell you more about attention than a single class-size number ever will.

3. Programs, curriculum, and choice

One genuine difference is specialization. Private schools can build their whole identity around one approach — Montessori, a faith tradition, an arts or athletics focus, or an enriched academic program. Public schools, meanwhile, offer more breadth than many parents realize: French Immersion and Extended French, regional programs in Peel for students with particular strengths or interests, and specialist high school programs. The Ontario curriculum is the backbone in both systems — private schools that grant Ontario Secondary School Diploma credits must follow it and are inspected by the Ministry of Education. So the question is rarely whether your child will learn the curriculum. It is which extras, which language options, and which school culture fit your child best.

4. Admissions, timing, and the application process

Public school admission is straightforward: your home address decides your neighbourhood school, and you register through the board. Private school admission is a process — applications, school visits, sometimes an interview or an entrance assessment for your child, and deadlines that often fall in the fall or winter before the entry year. If you are considering a private school for next September, this is the season to start: tour now, ask about deadlines, and find out whether there is a waitlist. And keep one thing in mind that brochures never say: a school that selects its students will naturally show stronger average results, because it chose who walked in the door. Judge a school by how much its students grow, not just by where its graduates go.

5. How to actually decide: five questions worth asking

Start with your child, not with the schools. First: what does your child need more of right now — challenge, confidence, structure, support, or simply a fresh start? Second: what does your family budget allow without strain, year after year, since switching mid-stream is disruptive? Third: what does the daily reality look like — commute, before- and after-school care, and your child's friendships? Fourth: what do teachers and current parents say when you visit, beyond the tour script? And fifth: what will you do if the first choice does not work out? Visit both a public and a private option with the same list of questions, watch how staff speak to children (not just to you), and trust what you see in real classrooms more than any ranking or brochure.

6. How tutoring can help

Here is something many Brampton parents discover: the gap they were hoping a new school would fix can often be closed where their child already is. If the real concern is reading fluency, math facts, or writing confidence, a few focused sessions with an Ontario-certified teacher — one-on-one or in a small group of no more than four — can target exactly that skill, without the cost and upheaval of changing schools. And if you do choose private school, tutoring can help your child walk into an entrance assessment or a new, faster-paced classroom feeling prepared rather than overwhelmed. Either way, it is worth finding out precisely where your child stands before making the biggest decision on the table.
