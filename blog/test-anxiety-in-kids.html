Test Anxiety in Kids: Signs and What Actually Helps

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 1, 2026
  • 6 Min

How to Spot It, Why It Happens, and the Calm-Down Strategies That Work

Your child knows the material. You watched them practise it at the kitchen table. Then the test comes back with a mark that does not match what they know — and they say their mind "went blank." If that sounds familiar, you are probably looking at test anxiety. It hits strong, conscientious students hardest, and it is very workable once you understand what it is and what actually helps.

1. What test anxiety actually is

When a child feels a test matters — and to them, it really does — the brain's alarm system can hijack the thinking brain. Working memory narrows, and the facts that were solid last night suddenly will not come. That "blanking out" is not laziness and it is not a lack of preparation. It is a nervous system doing its job a little too well. The key insight: the child is not choosing this. Knowing the material and being able to show it under pressure are two different skills.

2. The signs parents usually see at home

It shows up in behaviour: stomach aches or headaches on test mornings, trouble sleeping the night before, tears over homework that was fine yesterday, sudden statements like "I'm just dumb," and avoidance — the backpack that does not get unpacked, the form that never gets signed. Some kids over-prepare for hours while clearly not absorbing anything. If the pattern clusters around quizzes and tests rather than everyday schoolwork, you are likely looking at anxiety, not a learning gap.

3. What fuels it (and what quietly makes it worse)

Most test anxiety has three ingredients: pressure, perfectionism, and the fear of disappointing someone they love. Well-meaning habits at home can accidentally feed it. Constantly asking "did you study?" teaches a child that tests are threats to be survived. Comparing marks with siblings or cousins raises the stakes of every assessment. And reacting to a low mark with visible disappointment before curiosity teaches them that their worth rides on each test. Pressure comes from many places — peers, the child themselves — but the home atmosphere around tests is one of your most powerful levers.

4. Calm the body first, then the mind

You cannot reason an anxious body into calm; you have to signal safety to it first. Teach your child the 4-4-4 reset: breathe in for four counts, hold for four, out for four, and repeat three times. It directly slows the stress response, and a child who practises it at home can use it discreetly at their desk. On test mornings, keep routines predictable — same breakfast, same route, same send-off — because novelty adds stress. And protect sleep the night before a test: a tired brain blanking out looks exactly like anxiety.

5. Make test day feel familiar, not new

A huge part of test anxiety is the unfamiliarity of test conditions: a ticking clock, silent room, and questions seen for the first time. The antidote is rehearsal. Have your child do practice questions under timed conditions at home — even one short run-through changes the feeling of the real thing. When they make mistakes during practice, treat them as data, not drama: "This tells us exactly what to review tonight." Walk through a simple test-day plan together: read every question first, start with the easiest, skip and return, and never leave anything blank. A plan replaces panic with procedure. And after the test, ask "how did it feel?" before "how did it go."

6. Change the script they say to themselves

Listen for your child's inner commentary and you will hear the anxiety talking: "I'm going to fail," "everyone else finds this easy." Help them build replacement lines that feel true, not just positive. "I've practised this" beats "I'm amazing at tests" because the child actually believes it. A simple reset phrase for the moment of panic — something like "stop, breathe, start easy" — gives them a handle to grab when their mind goes blank. And be careful with praise that raises the bar: "you're so smart" can make every test feel like an identity check, while "you prepared well" keeps the focus on things they control.

7. When anxiety keeps showing up, build a bigger safety net

A little nervousness before a test is normal and even useful — it sharpens focus. But if the pattern persists week after week — the stomach aches, the blanking, the tears — bring in support rather than waiting for it to pass. Talk to your child's teacher, who can tell you whether the marks really match what they see in class and whether classroom accommodations are possible. And if anxiety is spilling into sleep, appetite, or daily life beyond tests, a family doctor or counsellor is the right next call. Getting help is how you stop a manageable problem from hardening into a lasting belief that tests are unbearable.

8. How tutoring can help

Test anxiety feeds on one big thing: the feeling of being underprepared. A tutor cannot remove nerves, but an Ontario-certified teacher can remove their fuel. In a small group or one-on-one session, a child rehearses the material — and the test format — so many times that test day feels familiar instead of threatening. No audience of thirty classmates, no silent gym, just guided practice until the method feels automatic. That kind of confidence is what actually quiets the inner critic on test day.

Test anxiety looks like a school problem, but it responds best to home support. Calm the body, rehearse the conditions, and change the script — and watch your child's marks start matching what they actually know.
