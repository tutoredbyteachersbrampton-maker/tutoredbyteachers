Should You Correct Every Mistake? Feedback That Helps vs. Hurts

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • November 13, 2026
  • 6 Min

Which Errors to Fix, Which to Leave, and How to Say It So Your Child Actually Listens

Your child slides their homework across the table, and your eye goes straight to the errors. The misspelled word, the wrong answer, the backwards letter. Your instinct is to fix all of them, right now, before the teacher sees it. It comes from a good place — you want your child to do well. But as a teacher, I can tell you something most parents never hear: correcting every mistake is one of the fastest ways to make a child stop trying. Here is how to tell the difference between feedback that helps and feedback that hurts.

1. The red-pen reflex: why correcting everything feels right

When you correct a mistake, you feel helpful. You caught the error, you fixed it, the work looks better. But from your child's side of the table, it can feel very different. A page covered in corrections reads like a message: "You got a lot wrong." Children who hear that message every night start to associate homework with criticism, and the easiest way to avoid criticism is to avoid trying — or to stop showing you their work at all. The goal of homework is not a perfect page. It is practice, and practice only works when a child is willing to keep going.

2. Not all mistakes are the same: slips vs. gaps

Before you correct anything, sort the errors into two piles. A slip is a mistake your child already knows how to avoid — a copied number wrong, a rushed spelling of a word they can spell, a skipped step in a problem they have done correctly before. Slips are worth a gentle nudge: "Read that one again slowly," or "Check your numbers here." A gap is different. Your child gets the same kind of question wrong every time, or they stare at it and genuinely do not know where to start. That is not a careless error — it is a missing piece of understanding, and no amount of red ink will fill it. Gaps need teaching, not correcting.

3. What happens when every error gets corrected

I see it in my classroom every year. The students whose parents correct everything at home are often the most anxious about making mistakes at school. They erase holes into their paper. They ask "Is this right?" after every single question instead of working through a problem. They would rather leave a question blank than risk being wrong. That is the real cost of over-correction: it teaches children that being wrong is something to fear, when in fact being wrong is how learning happens. A child who is not afraid to make a mistake will attempt harder questions, and attempting harder questions is exactly how they get better.

4. The feedback that actually helps: name what's right first

Good feedback always starts with what the child did well — and I mean specifically, not a vague "good job." Try: "You set this whole problem up perfectly — look, your columns are lined up and you carried the one correctly. Now let's look at this last step together." When a child hears exactly what they got right, two things happen: they know what to repeat next time, and they can actually hear the correction that follows without shutting down. Then pick one thing to fix — the most important one, not all of them. One correction per sitting is plenty. If there are five errors, choose the one that matters most and let the rest go. Your child's teacher will catch the rest, and that is literally our job.

5. A simple homework routine: spot it, explain it, redo it

When you do correct something, resist the urge to just give the right answer. That fixes the page but teaches nothing. Instead, use three steps. First, let your child spot it: "One of these answers doesn't look right — can you find it?" Children who find their own errors build the self-checking habit that good students rely on. Second, ask them to explain their thinking: "How did you get that answer?" Half the time, they will catch the mistake mid-sentence, and the fix becomes theirs, not yours. Third, have them redo it — right then, on the spot. A corrected answer written by a parent evaporates by morning. An answer your child reworked themselves is one they are far more likely to remember.

6. When to leave a mistake alone

Some mistakes are better left for the teacher. If your child is working on a brand-new concept and getting most of it wrong, that struggle is information — it tells their teacher exactly what needs reteaching. A homework page full of parent corrections hides that information, and the teacher may move on thinking the class understood. The same goes for spelling in a creative writing draft: if you fix every word, the teacher cannot see which spelling patterns your child has actually learned. And if homework has already turned into tears or a fight, stop. A finished page is never worth a damaged evening. Write a short note to the teacher explaining that your child tried and got stuck — teachers appreciate that far more than a perfect page.

7. How tutoring can help

Sometimes the hardest part of homework help is not the subject — it is the relationship. Children take correction from a teacher differently than from a parent, and a neutral, patient adult can say "let's look at this again" without it turning into a family argument. An Ontario-certified teacher can spot whether your child's errors are slips or gaps, fill the gaps with targeted practice, and teach your child how to check their own work. If homework has become a nightly battle in your house, that is exactly the kind of problem a free trial session can help you size up — honestly, with no pressure.

The next time your child hands you their homework, try this: find one thing they did well and say it out loud, then pick one mistake — just one — and help them find it themselves. You will be surprised how much more they are willing to try when the red pen stops feeling like a verdict. Mistakes are not the enemy of learning. They are the map that shows us where the learning still needs to happen.
