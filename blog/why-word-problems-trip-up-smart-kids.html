Why Word Problems Trip Up Smart Kids (and How Parents Can Help)

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 16, 2026
  • 6 Min

It's Usually Not the Math — It's the Reading Between the Lines

If your child can multiply 8 × 7 in their sleep but freezes the moment that same math appears inside a paragraph about pizza slices, you are not alone — and your child is not "bad at math." After years of teaching elementary and middle-school math, I can tell you this is one of the most common patterns I see in students who come to us for tutoring in Brampton. The surprising part? Most of these kids are perfectly capable of the calculation. What trips them up is something else entirely — and once you know what it is, you can start helping at home tonight.

1. A word problem is really a reading task wearing a math costume

Think about what a word problem actually asks a child to do. First, read a paragraph and understand what is happening. Then decide which of those words actually matter. Then translate the story into numbers and an operation. Only then do the actual calculation. That is four separate jobs, and the math itself is the easiest one. Research on math learning consistently shows that reading comprehension and working memory — not calculation speed — predict who struggles with word problems. So when your child says "I don't get it," they usually mean "I can't picture what this is asking." That is a completely different problem from "I can't do the math," and it needs a different kind of help.

2. The keyword trap: why hunting for clues backfires

Many kids are taught a shortcut: find a keyword, and it tells you the operation. "Altogether" means add, "left" means subtract, "each" means multiply. It feels like a clever trick — until it isn't. "How many are left?" does mean subtract, but "left the house" in the next sentence has nothing to do with subtraction. When a test writer rewords a problem slightly, the whole keyword system collapses, and the child who depended on it has nothing to fall back on. This is why I don't teach keywords as a strategy in my own classroom. Kids who rely on them guess the operation before they understand the story — and guessing is not thinking.

3. A few tricky words cause most of the damage

Sometimes the whole problem hinges on one phrase. "Fewer than" and "less than" flip a sentence backwards: "Sara has 5 fewer apples than Tom" sends many kids to subtract from Sara's number instead of Tom's. "Each" versus "total" decides between multiplication and division. Words like "remaining," "per," "twice as many," and "combined" each carry a precise meaning that children may never have been taught directly. Try this at home: read a word problem aloud with your child and pause at the key phrase. Ask, "What is this actually telling us?" You will be amazed how often the light bulb goes on before a single number is touched.

4. Too many numbers freeze the brain

A page full of numbers is intimidating, especially for younger students. Some problems even include extra information that isn't needed — and sorting the useful from the useless is a genuine skill. Teach your child to circle or underline only the numbers that relate to the question, then cross out the rest. One practical trick: cover the numbers entirely, read the story first, and ask "What is this problem asking me to find?" Then uncover the numbers and decide which ones belong. Removing the numbers removes the pressure to start calculating, and lets understanding come first.

5. A simple 4-step method you can teach tonight

You don't need a teaching degree — just a consistent routine. Step 1: Read the whole problem slowly, twice if needed. Step 2: Draw it — a quick sketch, bars, circles, or a number line. This is the step most kids skip and the one that helps the most. Step 3: Write the number sentence the drawing shows you. Step 4: Solve, then check — does the answer make sense in the story? Walk through two or three problems together using exactly these steps, in the same order every time, and praise the process, not the answer. Within a couple of weeks, many kids start reaching for the pencil to draw before they even think about calculating. That shift is the whole game.

6. Mistakes parents make when helping (with love)

The most common one is jumping to the operation too fast — "just add them!" — which teaches the child that math is about grabbing numbers rather than understanding stories. Another is showing them the way you learned it thirty years ago; Ontario's curriculum emphasizes visual models and number sense, and a method that clashes with what their teacher taught can double their confusion. And please don't correct every tiny error in one sitting — a child who gets five corrections on one problem stops taking risks on the next one. Help with the thinking, let the small arithmetic slips ride for now, and let their teacher's marking handle the rest.

7. How tutoring can help

Some word-problem struggles just need time and patience at the kitchen table. But if your child consistently shuts down, guesses wildly, or gets the calculation right but answers the wrong question, a few targeted sessions can reset the pattern. An Ontario-certified teacher can figure out exactly where the breakdown happens — reading, translating, or calculating — and rebuild the missing step with the visual models Ontario teachers actually use in class. A free trial session is a good no-pressure way to find out what's really going on, and what would help most.

Word problems feel hard because they ask your child to do the hardest part of math: think. But "think" is a skill, and skills can be taught. Read, draw, plan, check — practise that rhythm together a few times a week, and you may be surprised how quickly the freezing stops and the figuring starts.
