Scholarships in Canada: Where the Money Actually Is

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 15, 2026
  • 6 Min

The Types of Awards, the Deadlines That Matter, and How Your Teen Can Start Winning Money This Year

University tuition in Ontario keeps climbing, and most families I talk to assume scholarships are a lottery ticket for other people's kids — the straight-A student, the star athlete, the volunteer of the year. Here is what years in Ontario classrooms have taught me: a surprising amount of scholarship money goes to ordinary, hard-working students whose families simply knew where to look and when to apply. This is the plain-language map — the three kinds of scholarship money, the deadlines that matter most in Grade 12, and the mistakes that cost families thousands.

1. The three kinds of scholarship money

Almost every scholarship your teen will encounter falls into one of three buckets. Automatic entrance scholarships are awarded by the university itself, with no separate application — the offer of admission arrives with the money attached. Application-based awards require real work: essays, references, sometimes an interview. And local or community awards come from organizations in your own city — school boards, service clubs, businesses — and most families never hear about them. Understanding which bucket you're in tells you exactly what to do, so let's take them one at a time.

2. Automatic entrance scholarships: the easiest money you'll never apply for

This is the biggest, simplest source of scholarship money, and it runs entirely on Grade 12 marks. Most Ontario universities award entrance scholarships automatically based on the admission average — the same top-six average that decides admission. Each university publishes its award cutoffs on its financial aid page, and many list them on eINFO too. The pattern is straightforward: a higher average unlocks a higher award tier, and some awards renew each year if the student keeps their university grades up. Your teen does not write an essay or fill in a form for these — they just need the marks. That is why the fall of Grade 12 matters twice: midterm marks feed early offers, and final marks lock in both admission and the automatic award.

3. The big application-based awards: worth the essay effort

The second bucket takes work, and it is where serious money hides. National awards like the Loran Award, the Schulich Leader Scholarships, and the TD Scholarships for Community Leadership support students through multiple years of study — some worth tens of thousands of dollars. These are not decided by marks alone: they look for leadership, service, character, and a track record of initiative. Most open in the fall of Grade 12 and close between October and January, which means your teen should be shortlisting them in September and lining up references early — teachers need real notice to write a strong letter.

4. The tier most families miss: local and community awards

Here is the quiet secret of the scholarship world: the smaller the award's audience, the better your teen's odds. Brampton service clubs, community foundations, local businesses, unions, and many employers offer awards that draw far fewer applicants than the national names. Your school's guidance office keeps a list of these, and September is when to ask for it — many local deadlines land in the winter and spring. One more place families forget: your own workplace. Many employers offer scholarships for employees' children, and those get a fraction of the attention the national awards do.

5. OSAP and bursaries are not scholarships — apply anyway

Scholarships are merit money; OSAP is needs-based aid, and bursaries sit somewhere in between. I include them here because the number-one mistake I see is families skipping OSAP because they assume they won't qualify. Apply anyway. The assessment is free, it takes under an hour, and plenty of middle-income families are surprised by the result. Just as important: many university bursaries and grants require an OSAP application first, so skipping OSAP can quietly lock your teen out of other money. Bursaries often need only a short profile through the university's financial aid portal in the fall — mark it on the calendar the same week you submit the OUAC application.

6. A simple timeline your family can follow

Scholarships feel overwhelming until you put them on a calendar. September: visit the guidance office, pull the school's award list, and register on a scholarship search site like ScholarshipsCanada. October to December: draft the essays, ask teachers for references with at least three weeks' notice, and submit the national and university-specific applications. January: the OUAC deadline passes; keep working the list. February to April: local and community awards hit their deadlines — this is the window most families miss. May and June: admission offers arrive, automatic entrance awards attached. One spreadsheet — award name, deadline, requirements, status — is the whole system.

7. How tutoring can help

Here is the direct line between tutoring and scholarship money: automatic entrance awards are calculated from the Grade 12 top-six average, so every percentage point your teen gains this year can move them into a higher award tier — real money, attached to the admission offer. An Ontario-certified teacher can find exactly where the marks are leaking in the courses that count most and rebuild those skills while there is still time for it to matter. And for the application-based awards, the essays are read by strangers working through hundreds of submissions; a teacher who marks writing for a living can turn a rough draft into the kind of clear, specific story that survives the first cut. A few focused sessions this fall can pay for themselves many times over.

The scholarship game rewards two things: strong Grade 12 marks and organized applications. Start with the guidance office list in September, keep the spreadsheet, and treat deadlines like the precious things they are. Your teen does not need to be the top student in the province to win scholarship money — they just need to be in the running, on time, with a clear story to tell.
