How to Help Your Child Take Better Notes (5 Methods That Actually Work)

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 19, 2026
  • 6 Min

The Skill Nobody Teaches — and Why It Changes Everything

Here's a strange thing about school: we expect students to take notes from about Grade 5 onward, but almost nobody actually teaches them how. Most kids pick up one of two habits — writing down everything the teacher says, or writing down nothing at all. Both fail when the test arrives. Good note-taking is a genuine study tool: it forces the brain to process, organize, and remember. Here are five methods your child can start using this week, whether they're in elementary school or high school.

1. Notes are for thinking, not copying

The biggest shift is understanding what notes are for. Re-reading a textbook chapter feels productive but is one of the weakest ways to learn — the eyes move, but the brain coasts. Writing notes in your own words forces the brain to decide what matters, rephrase it, and connect it to something already known. That's why a student with one page of self-written notes usually outscores a student who re-read the chapter three times. Tell your child the golden rule: if the note is a sentence from the book, copied word for word, it doesn't count. Notes should sound like the student, not the textbook.

2. The Cornell method — the classroom classic

The Cornell method is the most researched note-taking format in schools, and it adapts beautifully for kids. Divide a page into three parts: a wide column on the right for notes taken during the lesson, a narrow column on the left for questions and key words added after class, and a strip across the bottom for a 2–3 sentence summary. During the lesson, your child writes main ideas in the right column. Within 24 hours, they add cue questions on the left ("What is a variable?" "Why do we reduce fractions?") and write the summary at the bottom. Come test time, they cover the right column and quiz themselves using only the questions. It turns a passive page into a self-made practice test — and it takes about ten minutes after each lesson.

3. Mind maps — for the big-picture thinkers

Some students don't think in lines — they think in pictures and connections. For them, mind maps work better than any column layout. Start with the main topic in the centre of a page (a history unit, a science chapter, a novel) and draw branches outward for the big ideas, then smaller branches for details. Use colours and simple drawings. Mind maps shine in subjects where everything connects: how Confederation, the railway, and western settlement relate in Grade 8 history, or how photosynthesis connects to food chains in Grade 6 science. They're also a lifesaver for review week, because one map can replace ten pages of linear notes for a student who reviews visually.

4. The 24-hour review — the step everyone skips

Notes only work if they're revisited — and timing matters more than most families realize. Memory researchers have shown that we lose most of what we learn within the first day unless we look at it again. The fix is a ten-minute review the same evening: read the day's notes, fill in gaps while the lesson is still fresh, and mark anything confusing with a star. That single short session can save hours of cramming later, because relearning forgotten material takes far longer than keeping it alive. Make it part of the homework routine, right after school or after dinner: notes out, ten minutes, stars where help is needed.

5. Build a personal shorthand and colour system

Students freeze up in class because they try to capture every word — and by the time they finish writing one sentence, the teacher is three ideas ahead. Give your child permission to be messy. Teach a few simple shortcuts: "w/" for with, "b/c" for because, arrows for cause and effect, stars for "this will be on the test." Then add a small colour code: yellow highlighter for definitions, pink for examples, blue for dates and formulas — whatever your child remembers. Three colours maximum, or the system becomes its own homework project. The goal isn't pretty notes. It's notes fast enough to keep up with a real classroom and clear enough to study from a month later.

6. How tutoring can help

If your child stares at a blank page in class or brings home notes that don't make sense the next day, an Ontario-certified teacher can teach the skill directly. In a few one-on-one or small-group sessions, a tutor can walk your child through the Cornell method on their actual current units, show them how to pick out test-worthy ideas, and build a review routine they can run on their own. Many students just need to see good note-taking modelled once on their own schoolwork — then the habit takes over. A free trial session is a good way to see where the skill gap is and fix it before the next round of tests.

Note-taking is one of those rare skills that pays off in every subject, at every grade level, for years. The students who figure it out early carry an advantage that has nothing to do with being "smart" — they just own their learning. Start small: pick one method, try it on one subject this week, and build from there. By report card season, your child won't just have notes. They'll have a study system.
