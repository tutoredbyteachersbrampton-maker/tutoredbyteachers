What Is an IEP? A Plain-Language Guide for Ontario Parents

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 4, 2026
  • 6 Min

No Jargon: What It Is, How It Works, and Your Role in It

If the school has mentioned an IEP for your child — in a meeting, on a phone call, or on a form you were asked to sign — and you left that conversation with more questions than answers, you are not alone. "Individual Education Plan" is one of those school terms everyone uses and almost nobody explains. Here is what it actually means, in plain language, from a teacher who has written and worked with these plans for years.

1. What an IEP actually is

An IEP — Individual Education Plan — is a written plan the school creates that spells out exactly how it will support your child: your child's strengths and learning needs, the specific ways teaching and assessment will be adjusted, and how progress will be tracked. Think of it less as a label and more as a game plan. It exists for students who need support beyond regular classroom instruction — and one thing surprises many parents: having an IEP does not automatically mean a lowered bar. Many students with IEPs work toward the exact same grade-level curriculum expectations as their classmates. The plan simply documents the supports that help them get there.

2. Your child does not need a formal diagnosis or label first

This is the misconception I clear up most often. In Ontario, a school can develop an IEP based on assessed learning needs — teacher observations, school-based assessments, and parent input — without your child going through the formal IPRC (Identification, Placement, and Review Committee) process that identifies a student as "exceptional." The IPRC is the formal committee route, with categories like Behaviour, Communication, Intellectual, Physical, and Multiple. The IEP is the day-to-day support document. If your child is struggling, the school does not have to wait for a label before putting supports in writing. That said, if your child's needs are significant, the IPRC route exists for a reason — ask the school when it would be appropriate.

3. The distinction that matters most: accommodations vs. modifications

These two words sound alike and mean very different things — and understanding them is the single most important thing in this whole guide. An accommodation changes HOW your child learns or shows what they know: extra time on tests, a quiet room for writing, assistive technology like text-to-speech, seating near the teacher, instructions broken into smaller steps. The curriculum expectations stay the same — your child is still working toward grade-level learning, just with a levelled playing field. A modification changes WHAT your child is expected to learn: grade-level expectations from the Ontario curriculum are replaced with expectations from a different grade level. This shows up on the report card and changes what a grade actually means. There is also a third category — alternative expectations — goals that aren't from the curriculum at all, like social skills, organization, or self-advocacy. Why this matters so much: accommodations keep every future pathway open. Modifications are sometimes genuinely the right call, but they change the meaning of the marks on the report card — so always be sure you know which one your child's plan uses, and why.

4. You are part of writing it — not just signing it

Ontario schools are required to consult parents — and the student, once they turn 16 — when developing an IEP. You should be notified that an IEP is being developed, asked for your input on your child's strengths and needs, and given a copy of the finished plan. Read the strengths section carefully: a good IEP starts from what your child can do, not just what they can't. And remember that the IEP is a living document, reviewed at least once per reporting period and updated as your child grows. If something in the plan stops matching your child, you can ask for it to be revisited — you don't have to wait for the next report card.

5. The questions worth asking before you sign

Walk into the IEP discussion with these five questions and you will understand the plan better than most parents do. First: which parts are accommodations and which are modifications — and why? Second: how will I know these supports are actually happening in the classroom? Third: what does progress look like this term, specifically — can you point to a measurable goal? Fourth: who do I contact if something in the plan isn't working? And fifth: what does this mean for EQAO testing — students with IEPs can receive accommodations on EQAO assessments, so ask how that will be handled for your child. Sign nothing you haven't read. Take the plan home, sleep on it, and ask your questions until the answers make sense.

6. How tutoring can help

An IEP describes the supports your child needs — but it doesn't always create the extra time needed to practise them. That's where targeted help fits in. A few one-on-one or small-group sessions with an Ontario-certified teacher can reinforce the exact strategies in the plan: the chunked instructions, the assistive-technology routines, the extra practice with reading or math foundations — at your child's pace, without the pressure of a full classroom. If your child's IEP includes accommodations like extra processing time, tutoring is where those supports get practised until they feel natural. And if modified expectations are in the plan, focused sessions can work precisely on those expectations so your child makes real progress on what the school has set out.

An IEP is not a verdict. It is the school saying, in writing, "here is how we will help." The parents who get the most out of it are the ones who read it, question it, and show up to every review — not as critics, but as the people who know their child best. When the plan and the parents pull in the same direction, the results show.
