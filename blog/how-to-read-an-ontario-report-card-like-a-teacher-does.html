How to Read an Ontario Report Card Like a Teacher Does

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 10, 2026
  • 6 Min

The Levels, the Learning Skills, and the Comments That Tell the Real Story

Fall progress reports are landing in backpacks across Brampton right about now, and every year I watch the same scene play out: a parent flips straight to the grades, sees a "B" or a "3," and either celebrates or panics — without ever reading the part of the report that matters most. Teachers actually read report cards in the opposite order from parents. Here is how we do it, so you can do it too.

1. Start with the Learning Skills and Work Habits

Every Ontario elementary report card opens with the six Learning Skills and Work Habits: Responsibility, Organization, Independent Work, Collaboration, Initiative, and Self-Regulation. Each is graded E (Excellent), G (Good), S (Satisfactory), or N (Needs Improvement). Teachers read this box first because it predicts everything else. A child with E's and G's here almost always grows academically; a child with S's and N's is telling you exactly where the ceiling is. If your child brings home a Level 2 in math but an E in Independent Work, that is a completely different conversation than a Level 2 paired with N in Organization. The skills box is the report's diagnosis — the grades are just the symptoms.

2. Decode what the levels actually mean

Ontario uses a four-level scale. Level 3 (70–79%) is the provincial standard — it means your child is meeting grade-level expectations, and that is a genuinely good place to be. Level 4 (80–100%) means achievement beyond the standard. Level 2 (60–69%) means approaching the standard, and Level 1 (50–59%) means achievement falling well below it. In Grades 1–6, subjects are reported with letter grades that map onto these bands; in Grades 7–8, you will see percentage marks. The biggest parent misunderstanding I see every year: a Level 3 is not "average" and not a reason to worry. It is the province's benchmark for success. A child sitting at Level 3 across the board is doing exactly what the curriculum expects.

3. Know which report you are holding

The fall document is a Progress Report, not a final report card, and it works differently. Instead of letter grades, it tells you whether your child is "progressing very well," "progressing well," or "progressing with difficulty" in each subject. Its job is to flag early and honestly: this is the teacher's way of telling you in October what needs attention before the real report card arrives in February. Take "progressing with difficulty" seriously — it is the teacher waving a flag while there is still time to act. The two Provincial Report Cards (roughly February and June) carry the formal grades. When the fall report raises a flag, the winter report should show the response.

4. Read the comments like evidence, not flattery

Teachers write comments in a specific shape: strengths first, then "next steps." The strengths part is warm-up. The next steps are the part that counts — that is where the teacher is telling you, plainly, what your child cannot yet do. Read for verbs. "Organizes ideas logically in paragraphs" is a different child than "is learning to organize ideas in paragraphs." Watch for repeated language across subjects: if the math comment and the language comment both mention rushing through work or unfinished tasks, you are not looking at a math problem — you are looking at a habit problem, and habits are fixable at home. And if a comment names a specific skill — regrouping, paragraph structure, decoding — that is your homework list, handed to you by a professional.

5. Compare the learning skills to the grades

The most useful reading of any report card is the mismatch. Strong learning skills but lower grades? Your child is likely a hard worker hitting a genuine curriculum gap — maybe fractions never clicked, or reading comprehension needs targeted practice. That is a teaching problem with a teaching solution. High grades but weak learning skills? That child is coasting on ability, and the report is warning you that the strategy will fail in harder grades — it usually does, around Grade 7 or 8. Both profiles show up in my classroom every year. The first needs content help; the second needs habits. Treating one like the other is the most common mistake parents make, and the report card is handing you the information to avoid it.

6. Bring three questions to the interview

Parent-teacher interviews follow the fall progress report for a reason: they exist so you can ask what the report means before the winter term is locked in. Do not walk in and ask, "How is my child doing?" — the report already answered that. Instead, ask: "What is the one skill that would move my child up the most this year?" Then: "What does that look like in practice at home — five minutes a night of what, exactly?" And finally: "What should I see changing by February?" Those three questions turn a polite ten-minute chat into a plan. Write down the answers. In February, when the term report arrives, you will know exactly what to compare it against — and so will your child.

7. How tutoring can help

A report card is a map, but somebody still has to walk the route. If the fall progress report flags "progressing with difficulty" in math, or the comments name a specific gap like fractions or reading comprehension, a few focused sessions with an Ontario-certified teacher can close it before February. We read report cards the way teachers read them — learning skills first, comments as evidence — and build the sessions around the actual next steps the teacher named. That is far more effective than generic homework help. The fall report exists to give you time. Using it is the whole point.

Flip the order next time a report comes home: learning skills first, then the levels, then the comments as your action plan. A report card is not a verdict on your child — it is a teacher handing you a map of exactly where they are and where to go next. Read it that way, and it stops being stressful and starts being useful.
