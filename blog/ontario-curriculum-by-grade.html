The Ontario Curriculum by Grade: What Your Child Is Expected to Learn in Math & English

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • September 23, 2026
  • 8 Min

A Certified Teacher's Plain-Language Guide, Division by Division

If you are a parent in Brampton, you have probably thought this exact sentence at least once: "I have no idea what they're supposed to know by June." Report cards arrive with letter grades and level numbers, but they rarely tell you what your child was actually expected to learn — and whether they are on track. As an Ontario-certified teacher, I read the curriculum documents so you don't have to. Below is a plain-language, grade-by-grade map of what the Ontario curriculum expects in math and English, from Kindergarten through Grade 10, plus how the big provincial tests fit into the picture.

1. Kindergarten to Grade 3: The Foundations

Everything else rests on these years. In English, children learn phonics — connecting letters to the sounds they make — then move from sounding out words to reading short books with real fluency by Grade 3. They write simple sentences, spell common words correctly, and start organizing their ideas into a beginning, middle, and end. In math, the focus is number sense: understanding what numbers mean, not just reciting them. Children count, compare numbers, and learn to add and subtract. They measure, sort, and recognize patterns and simple shapes. This is also when the first EQAO assessment arrives, at the end of Grade 3 — a snapshot of whether these foundation skills are in place. If your child is still struggling to sound out words or add without finger-counting by the end of Grade 3, that is the moment to act — every later grade assumes these skills are automatic.

2. Grades 4 to 6: Building Blocks

Now the curriculum starts layering on complexity. In math, students must memorize their multiplication facts — times tables become the engine for everything that follows — and they learn long division, multi-digit multiplication, and fractions: comparing them, adding and subtracting them, and linking them to decimals and percentages. In English, students graduate to reading full chapter books, learning to find the main idea, make inferences, and back up their answers with evidence from the text. Writing moves into real paragraphs: a clear topic sentence, supporting details, and an ending sentence. The second EQAO assessment lands at the end of Grade 6, measuring whether students have absorbed these building blocks. Many families arrive in this window, because a shaky times-table base or weak reading comprehension in Grade 4 quietly becomes a much bigger problem by Grade 6. If multiplication facts aren't fluent yet, our times tables guide walks through how to build that fluency at home.

3. Grades 7 and 8: The Middle Years

This is where math takes a noticeable jump. Students meet integers — negative numbers and the rules for adding, subtracting, and multiplying them — and begin algebra: working with variables, solving simple equations, and spotting patterns in number sequences. Geometry and measurement get more demanding too, with area, volume, and relationships that prepare students for high school. In English, writing stretches into full essays with an introduction, body paragraphs, and a conclusion — covered in depth in our essay writing guide. Reading shifts from "what happened" to "what does it mean": students think critically about a text's purpose, compare viewpoints, and support opinions with evidence. Vocabulary grows fast, and spelling and grammar conventions tighten. Students who coasted on natural ability often hit their first real wall here, just as homework loads rise and study habits start to matter as much as raw knowledge.

4. Grade 9: High School Begins — Destreamed

Grade 9 is a fresh start, and an important one: math in Ontario is now destreamed, so every student takes the same Grade 9 math course (MTH1W), whether they found math easy or hard in Grade 8. The course deepens Grade 8 algebra — solving and graphing linear equations, working with exponents and expressions — and adds data literacy and financial math students will use in real life. There are no separate academic and applied streams anymore, the range of starting points in each classroom is wider — and students who are behind must keep up with the same pace as everyone else. Grade 9 English focuses on reading a range of texts — novels, short stories, poems, media — and writing in several forms with clear organization and control of language. This is also an EQAO year: students write the Grade 9 EQAO math assessment. Our Grade 9 math guide breaks down the destreamed course in more detail if this is your child's year.

5. Grade 10: The Literacy Year

Grade 10 math builds on Grade 9 with deeper algebra, quadratics, and more advanced problem solving. But the headline of Grade 10 is English — or more precisely, the OSSLT. The Ontario Secondary School Literacy Test is written in Grade 10 and is tied to graduation: students must meet the province's literacy requirement to earn their diploma. It tests the reading and writing skills students are expected to have by the end of Grade 9 — news reports, opinion pieces, and comprehension questions — and our OSSLT guide walks through the whole test. If your child is in Grade 10 this year, the fall administration window runs November 3 to 30, 2026. In English class itself, students read longer, more complex texts and write more sophisticated essays, including literary analysis. A student who struggles with essay structure or reading comprehension in Grade 10 is not just having a tough year — they are heading into an exam that counts.

6. How EQAO and the OSSLT map onto all of this

Think of the provincial assessments as checkpoints, not the destination. EQAO tests are written at the end of Grades 3 and 6 (reading, writing, and math) and in Grade 9 (math only). Each one checks whether students have met the curriculum expectations for that stage: the foundations, the building blocks, and the middle-years math. The OSSLT in Grade 10 then checks the literacy skills needed for graduation. None of these tests introduce surprise topics; they sample what the curriculum says students should know. That is why missing grade-level expectations is the real worry, not the test itself: a child who is genuinely on track will recognize everything on it. When scores come back low, they are usually flagging a gap that opened up one or two grades earlier — which brings us to the most practical question of all.

7. How to find out exactly where your child stands

The honest truth: a report card grade won't tell you which specific skills are missing. A "C" in math could mean times tables, fractions, word problems, or all three — and each needs a different fix. The way teachers figure this out is a skills assessment: working through problems at and around grade level to pinpoint exactly which curriculum expectations are solid and which are shaky. If you've been wondering where the gaps might be, our list of signs a child needs a tutor is a good self-check — and that is exactly what our free first session does. An Ontario-certified teacher meets your child — online or in person in Springdale, Brampton — works through grade-level material, and gives you a straight answer: where they stand against the expectations above, what the priority gaps are, and whether tutoring would actually help. No pressure, no jargon — just this guide's clarity, personalized to your child.

The Ontario curriculum is a long staircase, and every step assumes the one below it. Knowing what each grade expects takes the mystery out of report cards — and once you can see where your child stands on the staircase, helping them climb becomes a much simpler job.
