Why Does My Child Forget Everything They Studied? The Science of Spaced Practice

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 9, 2026
  • 6 Min

What Memory Research Says About Cramming — and the Simple Routine That Beats It

You have seen it happen: your child studies for hours the night before a test, seems to know the material at bedtime, and then blanks on half the questions the next morning. Here is the good news — your child is not lazy and their memory is not broken. They were simply using a study method that was never going to work. Memory research has shown for over a century that cramming fades fast, while spreading study sessions out over time locks knowledge in. It is called spaced practice, and it is one of the most reliable findings in all of learning science.

1. Why cramming feels productive but fails

Cramming feels productive because recognition is easy. After re-reading a chapter three times, everything looks familiar — and that fluency tricks the brain into thinking, "I know this." But recognition is not recall. On a test, nobody is reading the answers off a page; your child has to pull the information out of their own head. Cramming stuffs facts into short-term memory, which is why the material is there at bedtime and gone by morning. Spaced practice works on a completely different principle: it is the act of retrieving information after it has started to fade that strengthens the memory each time.

2. The forgetting curve — and why spacing flattens it

In the late 1800s, psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus mapped how quickly we forget new information. The pattern he found has held up ever since: memory drops steeply in the first day after learning something, then levels off. But here is the key part — every time you successfully recall that information before it fully fades, the forgetting slows down. The second review is easier than the first, the third easier still, and each retrieval makes the memory last longer. That is why reviewing a concept one day later, then three days later, then a week later, builds a memory that survives until test day — and well past it.

3. Retrieval practice: the partner technique that doubles the effect

Spaced practice tells you when to study; retrieval practice tells you how. It means studying by pulling information out rather than putting it in — flashcards, practice questions, and blank-page "brain dumps" of everything your child can remember all count. Try this at the kitchen table: have your child read one section, close the book, and tell you out loud what they remember. Then open the book and check what they missed. The parts they struggled to retrieve are the parts they actually learned in that moment.

4. A weekly routine any family can actually follow

You do not need a complicated system. For each subject, aim for three short sessions across the week rather than one long one the night before. A realistic rhythm: learn the material in class or at the desk on Day 1; on Day 2 or 3, do a 10-minute closed-book review — flashcards, practice questions, or explaining it aloud; a few days later, do another 10-minute recall round mixing in older topics too. Keep sessions short and focused — 20 to 25 minutes for elementary students, a little longer for high school — with breaks in between. A simple calendar or a sheet on the fridge where your child ticks off each session turns spacing into a habit instead of a battle. Consistency matters far more than perfection: even imperfect spacing beats perfect cramming.

5. Mixing it up: why interleaving beats blocking

There is one more trick the research supports. Instead of doing 20 multiplication questions, then 20 division questions, then 20 fraction questions (called blocking), mix the question types together in the same session — a few of each, shuffled. This is called interleaving, and it works because tests do not announce which kind of question is coming. Blocked practice lets kids coast on the pattern; interleaved practice forces them to decide which strategy to use every single time — the actual skill the test demands. It feels harder in the moment, but that struggle is what builds flexible understanding.

6. The honest trade-off: effort now, freedom later

Here is what I tell parents plainly: spaced practice is not less work overall, but it feels different. Instead of one stressful all-nighter before a test, your child does several short, calm reviews across the week. The payoff is real — no more panicked nights, and knowledge that is still there at report-card time and on the EQAO in May. With report cards coming in October/November, the next few weeks are the perfect time to build this habit: start with one subject, keep the sessions short, and let the first improved test grade do the convincing.

7. How tutoring can help

The hardest part of spaced practice is not the science — it is the accountability. A tutor session naturally creates spacing: material is learned, then revisited in the next session, then pulled back out again a week later, with an Ontario-certified teacher making sure each retrieval is done properly. When we work with a student, we do not just cover the week's content — we cycle older material back into every session, building in exactly the retrieval routine described above without your family having to manage it alone. If your child studies hard but still cannot seem to hold on to what they learned, a free trial session is a good way to find out what is going on and what would help.

Your child does not need a better memory — they need a better method. Spread the studying out, make every session about pulling information out rather than putting it in, and mix topics instead of doing them in blocks. It is simple, it is backed by more than a century of research, and it starts working the very first week.
