Why Mental Math Still Matters in the Calculator Age

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • November 7, 2026
  • 6 Min

The Skill Behind Every Answer — and How to Build It at Home

Your child is doing math homework with a calculator open on the kitchen table. They type in 8 × 7, get the answer, and write it down — but the calculator did the work, and their brain never joined in. That is the quiet problem with relying on calculators too early: your child can produce answers without ever understanding numbers. Mental math is not about doing sums in your head to impress anyone. It is the foundation every other math skill is built on.

1. Calculators give answers; mental math gives understanding

A calculator tells you that 48 ÷ 6 is 8. Mental math is what lets your child know that 8 is a reasonable answer — that 48 is close to 50, and 50 ÷ 6 is a little more than 8. That sense of what an answer should look like is called number sense, and it is the skill that catches mistakes. A student with strong number sense notices if they typed the wrong number, because the answer feels wrong. A student without it copies down whatever the screen says, even if it is off by a factor of ten.

2. What the Ontario curriculum actually expects

The Ontario math curriculum does not treat mental math as an old-fashioned extra. From the early grades on, students are expected to use mental math strategies — adding by breaking numbers apart, doubling and halving, using friendly numbers like 10 and 100, and recalling basic facts. In Grades 4 to 6, students are expected to add, subtract, multiply, and divide whole numbers using mental strategies, and to estimate before they calculate. That is why the EQAO tests in Grades 3 and 6 — this year, May 4 to June 8, 2027 — are far easier for students who can work with numbers in their heads. Calculators are allowed in some parts of the Grade 9 EQAO math assessment, but the students who do best are still the ones whose mental math is strong enough that they barely need one.

3. Where weak mental math shows up first

Mental math gaps rarely announce themselves as a math problem. They show up as slow homework, because every small calculation takes a detour through a calculator. They show up as trouble with fractions in Grade 6 and 7, because a child who cannot see that 3 × 4 is 12 will struggle to see that 3/4 of 12 is 9. They show up in word problems, because a child still working hard on the arithmetic has no brainpower left for figuring out what the problem is asking. And they show up on tests, where the clock is ticking and there is no time to punch in every number. If your child says "I'm just bad at math," it is often not math they are bad at — it is the mental arithmetic underneath it, and that is very fixable.

4. Five minutes a day is enough

Mental math is built the same way muscles are: a little bit, often. Five minutes a day beats an hour once a week. Try doubling games in the car — "double 14, now double that" — or ask your child to add the prices on the grocery shelf as you walk down the aisle. Play dice or card games that force quick adding; a simple game of "make 10" with a deck of cards practises the single most useful fact family in elementary math. For older kids, try estimating first: before they calculate 38 × 6, ask them to guess roughly what it will be, then check. The goal is never speed for its own sake — it is a child who looks at numbers and sees relationships instead of a wall of digits.

5. Everyday math is the best practice there is

The best mental math practice does not look like homework at all. Cooking is fractions in disguise: halving a recipe, measuring three-quarters of a cup, figuring out how many minutes until dinner is ready. Money is mental math in real life: "You have $10, the snack is $3.50 — how much change should you get?" Time is mental math too: "We leave at 4:15 and it takes 25 minutes — what time do we arrive?" When your child practises math inside real situations, the facts stick, because they are tied to something that matters. And your child starts to see math as a tool they already use, not a subject that only lives in a workbook.

6. When it is more than a calculator habit

Some children lean on calculators because it is easier, and a few weeks of short daily practice fixes it. But if your child still counts on their fingers for basic facts in Grade 4 or 5, freezes when a problem has no calculator nearby, or takes far longer than classmates on the same work, it may be a genuine gap in number sense rather than a habit. That is exactly the kind of thing the fall progress reports in October and November are meant to flag — look at the learning skills and the math comments, not just the level. And ask the teacher one direct question at your parent-teacher interview: is my child fluent with basic facts, or are they still working them out every time? The earlier a gap is found, the smaller it is to close.

7. How tutoring can help

Mental math is one of the fastest things to improve with the right help, because it responds so well to short, focused practice. An Ontario-certified teacher can find exactly which facts and strategies your child is missing, then build them up with quick drills, games, and real-life problems — no long worksheets, no pressure. Once the basic facts become automatic, everything else in math gets easier: homework takes less time, word problems feel less overwhelming, and tests stop being a race against the clock.

Calculators are not the enemy — your child will use them for the rest of their life. But a tool is only useful in the hands of someone who understands what it is doing. Five minutes a day of mental math, built into the car ride, the grocery store, and the kitchen, gives your child the number sense that makes every calculator answer trustworthy.
