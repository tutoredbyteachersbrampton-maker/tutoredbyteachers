Why Your Child's Math Marks Dropped in High School (and What to Do)

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 8, 2026
  • 6 Min

The Elementary-to-High-School Math Gap Is Real — and Fixable

One of the most common things I hear from parents sounds like this: "She got 85s in Grade 8 math — now she's getting 62 in Grade 9. What happened?" If this is your family, please hear this first: your child is not suddenly bad at math. The drop is almost never about intelligence or effort. High school math is a different game from elementary math, and most students walk in without being told the rules changed. Here is why the marks fall — and what brings them back up.

1. High school math is genuinely a different subject

In elementary school, math is mostly procedures: add, subtract, multiply, divide, follow the steps. Grade 9 math (MTH1W in Ontario) shifts to algebra — letters mixed with numbers, unknowns on both sides of equations, graphs of linear relations. This abstract thinking is new for every student. A child who was fast and accurate with arithmetic can feel lost when the numbers turn into x's and y's. That confusion is normal — it means they need a new kind of thinking, not that they lost the old skills.

2. Old cracks in the foundation show up now

Algebra is built on top of elementary skills — and any gaps there become visible fast. Fractions are the biggest one: if your child was shaky on adding fractions with different denominators or simplifying, nearly every equation becomes harder, because algebra is full of fractional coefficients. Integer rules (multiplying and dividing negatives), the order of operations, and basic multiplication facts all behave the same way: Grade 8 homework could hide them, but Grade 9 tests expose them. When I tutor a struggling Grade 9 student, we almost always start by quietly repairing one or two of these foundation skills — and the algebra gets dramatically easier.

3. Tests count more — and homework counts less

In elementary school, much of the report card mark comes from classwork, assignments, and participation. Students who did everything neatly could earn strong marks even with average test scores. In high school math, the course mark is typically weighted with the large majority coming from term work — and term work in math means tests and quizzes, plus a final exam or culminating task. There is nowhere to hide. A student who earned 85 through effort and completion now gets 65, because the only thing being measured is what they can do alone, under time pressure. The fix is not more homework completion — it is genuine test-ready understanding.

4. The pace doubled and nobody said stop

A high school math class covers roughly a full unit every two to three weeks. Miss a day, misunderstand one key lesson, or need extra time on a concept, and the class has already moved on. Math is the most cumulative subject there is — you cannot factor if you are shaky on expanding, and you cannot graph lines confidently if solving equations is wobbly. Falling two lessons behind feels like falling a month behind, because everything after assumes the earlier part. The rule I give every student: if you don't understand today's lesson, ask about it within 24 hours. Small confusions, handled the same day, never grow into failing test scores.

5. The study habits that worked in Grade 8 don't transfer

Most students arrive in high school studying math the wrong way: they re-read their notes, look over textbook examples, and feel prepared — then the test comes and they blank. Here is the truth every math teacher knows: math is a performance skill, not a reading skill. You cannot learn algebra by reading about algebra, any more than you can learn piano by reading about piano. The only studying that works is doing problems — fresh problems, without looking at the solutions, under mild time pressure. Twenty minutes of real problem-solving beats two hours of note-reviewing. If your child says "I studied for hours," check whether those hours were spent doing math or just looking at math.

6. What you can do right now, this week

Start with the most recent test — not to argue about the mark, but to diagnose it. Spread it on the table and sort the errors: careless mistakes (wrong sign, copied the number wrong) versus concept mistakes (no idea where to start). Careless errors need slower, more deliberate practice; concept errors point to the exact lessons to revisit. Next, ask the teacher one question: "Which unit is coming up next, and what should my child review to be ready?" Teachers love this — it shows you want to support the learning, not dispute the mark. Then set up fifteen to twenty minutes of daily math practice: not re-reading, but doing five to ten fresh problems from that unit. Finally, start an error log — a notebook where your child redoes each missed test question, correctly, with the steps shown.

7. How tutoring can help

A dropped math mark in Grade 9 is almost always a timing problem, not a talent problem — the longer gaps sit, the wider they get, because the class never waits. An Ontario-certified teacher can pinpoint exactly where the foundation cracked, repair it in a few focused sessions, and teach your child how to study math the way high school demands: problem-solving practice, error logs, test-day strategies. With fall progress reports approaching, a few sessions in October can be the difference between a discouraging November report card and a confident one.

The dip from elementary to high school math is one of the most predictable patterns in education — and one of the most reversible. The students who recover are not the ones who got smarter; they are the ones who learned the new rules: fix the foundation, do problems instead of reading them, and ask for help the same day confusion strikes. Your child's 85-student is still in there — they just need a bridge to the other side.
