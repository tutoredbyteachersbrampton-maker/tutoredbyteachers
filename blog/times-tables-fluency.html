Times Tables Without Tears: Building Math Fact Fluency

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • September 23, 2026
  • 6 Min

Fluency Through Patterns and Play, Not Tears at the Kitchen Table

If the words "times tables" give you a little flash of dread, you are not alone. Many of us sat through painful rote drills as kids — chanting rows out loud, getting quizzed under pressure, and feeling our stomach drop when we blanked on 7 × 8. Nobody enjoyed it, and for most of us, it did not stick the way teachers hoped. If you are a Brampton parent watching your child wrestle with multiplication right now, here is the good news: there is a better way, and it does not involve tears — yours or theirs. As a certified Ontario teacher, I teach times tables through patterns, games, and tiny daily habits. This guide walks you through exactly how.

1. Why fluency actually matters

Math fact fluency means your child can recall multiplication facts like 6 × 7 or 8 × 9 quickly and accurately, without counting on their fingers every time. It matters because multiplication is the key that unlocks everything that comes after it in the Ontario curriculum: simplifying and comparing fractions in Grades 4 and 5, long division, multi-digit multiplication, and every multi-step word problem in between. When a child stalls on the facts, every new topic feels twice as hard — not because they cannot understand the concept, but because their working memory is busy doing basic arithmetic instead of learning the new idea. I say this to parents every year: a child who struggles with math is rarely "bad at math." Very often, they are just stuck on their facts, and once the facts click, the whole subject opens up.

2. Start with patterns, not memorization

Here is the shift that changes everything: do not teach the times tables as 100 random facts to memorize. Teach them as patterns, and teach the easiest patterns first. The 0s, 1s, 2s, 5s, and 10s are the friendly tables — kids can learn them through skip-counting in a few days, and they follow obvious rules (the 5s always end in 5 or 0, the 10s just add a zero). Once those are solid, introduce the idea of commutativity: 3 × 6 is the same as 6 × 3. That one insight nearly halves the remaining work, because every new fact only needs to be learned once. Children who see the patterns do not feel like they are memorizing a mountain — they feel like they are learning tricks, which is a completely different emotional experience.

3. The 9s finger trick (and other patterns kids love)

The 9s are famously the wall where children get stuck — until you show them the finger trick, and then it becomes the table they show off. Here is how it works: hold up both hands with all ten fingers spread. To multiply 9 by a number, fold down that numbered finger, counting from the left. For 9 × 4, fold down finger 4 — you will have 3 fingers up on the left and 6 on the right, which makes 36. It works every time from 9 × 1 to 9 × 10. Kids are amazed, and amazement is a wonderful motivator. Other patterns work the same magic: in the 9s, the digits of each answer always add up to 9 (54 → 5 + 4 = 9). In the 4s, every answer is just double the 2s. When children discover the shortcuts themselves, the facts stop being enemies and start being puzzles.

4. Games that actually work

Practice does not have to be painful to be effective — it just has to be frequent. A deck of playing cards is one of the best math tools you own: flip two cards and multiply them (pull out the face cards or give them values of 10). A pair of dice works the same way and takes thirty seconds to start. Skip-counting songs get the facts into long-term memory through rhythm. Short daily sprints also work well: set a one-minute timer and see how many facts your child can answer, then try to beat yesterday's number. A child who will happily play cards for ten minutes will shut down after two minutes of a worksheet asking for the same facts.

5. A simple 5-minute daily routine

Consistency beats intensity every time. Here is a printable-style routine any parent can follow in five minutes a day. Step one: pick ONE fact family to work on this week — for example, the 6s. Just the 6s. Step two: spend the first two minutes reviewing facts your child already knows, mixing in a few of the new ones. Step three: spend the last three minutes on the new family, one or two facts at a time, using patterns (6 × 5 is just the 5s table, so 6 × 5 = 30, and 6 × 6 is one more group of 6). Step four: end with a quick win — finish on a fact your child can nail, so practice ends on confidence instead of frustration. Five minutes a day, one family at a time, always mixing old facts with new. Within a month, parents are usually surprised at how far their child has come.

6. Common mistakes to avoid

The two mistakes I see most often both come from rushing. First: timed tests too early. Timed quizzes have their place — eventually, speed does matter — but introducing them before the facts are solid teaches children to panic, not to recall. Confidence comes first, speed comes later. Second: jumping around randomly. Drilling every table at once, from 0 × 0 to 12 × 12, overwhelms a child's memory and guarantees nothing sticks. It feels productive because the flashcards are flying, but it is the multiplication equivalent of trying to learn a whole language in one lesson. Work systematically — friendly tables first, then one fact family at a time — and the facts accumulate. Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast.

7. When to get extra help

Most children build fluency with patterns, games, and a few minutes of daily practice. But if your child is reaching the middle elementary years — around Grades 4 or 5 — and still cannot recall the basic facts despite steady practice, that is a sign to get support sooner rather than later. The reason is mathematical, not emotional: Grades 4 and 5 in Ontario pile fractions, division, and multi-step problem solving on top of multiplication. A child who is still counting 7 × 8 on their fingers will spend all their mental energy on arithmetic and have none left for the new concept. A certified teacher can spot exactly where the breakdown is and build a plan that fixes the foundation instead of papering over it.

8. Fluency is a skill, and skills can be built

Nobody is born knowing their times tables, and nobody is born unable to learn them. Fluency is built the same way every skill is built: small steps, patterns that make sense, repetition that feels like play, and enough patience to let it sink in. If you remember one thing from this guide, let it be this — the goal is not to drill your child until they cry. The goal is to hand them a toolbox of patterns and games so the facts feel discoverable, not imposed. The tears-and-chanting era of times tables is over, and good riddance. Your child can learn this, and you can help them learn it, five minutes a day.

If you have been trying the flashcards, the apps, and the worksheets and the facts still are not sticking, sometimes a fresh set of eyes helps. A certified teacher can diagnose exactly which fact families are missing, teach the patterns in a way that clicks for your child, and make practice something your child actually looks forward to.
