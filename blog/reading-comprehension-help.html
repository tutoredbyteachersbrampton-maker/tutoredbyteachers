How to Improve Your Child's Reading Comprehension at Home

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • September 23, 2026
  • 6 Min

They Read the Words Fine — But Can't Tell You What They Read

It is one of the most common worries I hear from Brampton parents: "My child reads the words fine, but then can't remember what they read." Your child finishes a chapter, you ask what it was about, and you get a blank stare — or a guess that has nothing to do with the story. It feels confusing because the reading itself sounds good. But here is what every Ontario-certified teacher knows: reading the words and understanding the words are two different skills. The good news is that comprehension is teachable, and you can start building it at home tonight with a simple routine — before, during, and after reading.

1. First, figure out which problem you're seeing

Not every reading struggle is a comprehension problem, and the fix depends on which one your child has. A decoding problem means your child can't read the words smoothly — they stumble over words, guess at them, or read very slowly and sound frustrated. A comprehension problem looks different: your child reads aloud fluently, maybe even quickly, but can't answer "What was that about?" Try this tonight: after a page, ask your child to close the book and tell you what happened in their own words. If they can't, you're looking at a comprehension gap. If they never even got the words right in the first place, the barrier is decoding. Both are fixable, but they need different help — decoding needs practice with sounds and word patterns, while comprehension needs the thinking routine below. Getting this right first saves you from working on the wrong thing.

2. Before reading: preview, predict, and set a purpose

Good readers do three minutes of work before they start reading — and you can teach your child to do the same. First, preview the text together: look at the cover, the title, the headings, and the pictures. For nonfiction, skim the bolded words and any charts or diagrams. Second, ask your child to predict: "What do you think this will be about?" There is no wrong answer here — predicting wakes up the brain and connects the new text to what your child already knows. Third, set a purpose: give the reading a mission. "Let's read to find out why the main character is nervous," or "Let's see if we can learn three new facts about space." A child who reads with a question in mind is a child who reads actively, and active readers remember far more than passive ones.

3. During reading: pause and retell

This is the single most powerful habit in this whole routine, so make it a non-negotiable: stop regularly and retell. Every page or two — or at the end of each paragraph in a nonfiction text — pause and ask, "Tell me what just happened, in your own words." Retelling in their own words is what proves understanding; simply repeating sentences back is just echo. While you're pausing, ask small questions too: Who is this about? What went wrong? Why do you think the character did that? For fiction, try visualization: "What does that scene look like in your head?" Kids who picture the story as they read understand it better and remember it longer. The goal is to break the habit of eyes-moving-without-brain-engaged, which is exactly what produces that blank stare at the end of a chapter.

4. After reading: summarize, connect, and discuss

When the reading is done, the learning is not. First, ask for a one-sentence summary: "What was the whole thing about, in one sentence?" If your child can do this, comprehension happened. If they can't, go back and retell section by section together — that is where the understanding gets rebuilt. Next, help them make connections: to their own life ("Has something like this ever happened to you?"), to another book or movie ("This reminds me of..."), or to the wider world ("Why does this matter?"). The Ontario curriculum explicitly teaches these connections, so you're building exactly what school expects. Finally, discuss — not quiz. Ask for opinions: "Was the character right to do that?" "What was your favourite part?" A child who argues about a book is a child who understood it.

5. Three simple tools that make it stick

You don't need workbooks or apps for this — three low-tech tools do the job. First, graphic organizers: a simple story map drawn on paper (characters, setting, problem, solution) for fiction, or a main-idea-and-details web for nonfiction. Kids who can't "hold" a text in their heads can hold it on paper, and that's often the breakthrough. Second, a vocabulary notebook: when your child meets a word they don't know, write it down, look it up together, and have them write it in their own words plus use it in a sentence. Understanding collapses when too many words are unknown, so this directly protects comprehension. Third, read aloud together — even with older kids. Take turns reading paragraphs, and think aloud as you go: "I wonder why she said that..." Hearing you model the thinking teaches your child what good readers do inside their heads.

6. What the Ontario curriculum actually expects

This routine isn't just nice parenting advice — it mirrors what Ontario schools are required to teach and measure. The Ontario language curriculum expects students to use comprehension strategies like predicting, making inferences, connecting texts to their own experiences, and reflecting on what they read. The EQAO reading assessments in Grades 3, 6, and 9 ask students to find what a text says directly, figure out what it implies, and make connections to the bigger picture — exactly the pause-and-retell, predict, and connect skills from this routine. And in Grade 10, the OSSLT reading section tests the same three abilities on everything from news reports to graphic texts. So every night you practise this routine, you're preparing your child not just for tomorrow's class, but for the assessments Ontario uses to measure them.

7. When the routine isn't enough, get a second pair of eyes

Most children respond to this routine within a few weeks of steady use — retelling gets easier, summaries get sharper, and that blank stare starts to disappear. But sometimes the gap doesn't close, and that is useful information, not failure. If your child still can't retell after consistent practice, if they actively avoid reading or get upset when asked about it, or if you realize the real barrier was decoding all along, a trained eye can pinpoint exactly where the breakdown is happening. An Ontario-certified teacher can assess whether the issue is vocabulary, fluency, inferencing, or something else entirely — and work on precisely that, one-on-one or in a small group, online or in person. You don't have to diagnose it alone.

Reading comprehension isn't a talent some children have and others don't — it's a set of habits, and habits can be built. Preview before, pause during, summarize after, and keep a few simple tools on hand. Start tonight with just one book and one retelling, and you'll already know more about how your child reads than you did yesterday.
