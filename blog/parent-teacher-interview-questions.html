Parent-Teacher Interviews: 10 Questions Every Ontario Parent Should Ask This Fall

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • September 26, 2026
  • 6 Min

How to Make Fifteen Minutes Count

Fall parent-teacher interviews are around the corner, and most parents walk in with one question — "how's my child doing?" — and walk out with a polite answer that tells them almost nothing. Fifteen minutes is short, but it's enough if you spend it on the right questions. Here's how to make yours count.

1. Why fall interviews matter more than you think

In Ontario elementary schools, the fall progress report lands in October or November, and parent-teacher interviews usually follow right behind it. This is the earliest honest checkpoint of the school year — and the most useful one. A concern raised in November can be fixed by February. The same concern raised on the February report card is already a rescue mission. Teachers also form their first real impressions of your child in these opening weeks: how they learn, where they hesitate, what motivates them. Walking into that conversation prepared means you leave with a plan, not just a handshake.

2. Do five minutes of homework before you go

You don't need a binder. You need three things: your child's recent work (flip through the backpack for graded assignments or tests), one thing you've noticed at home ("homework takes two hours and ends in tears" is gold), and one question you actually want answered. Teachers see dozens of parents in a night; the parents who get the most out of it are the ones who arrive with something specific. If both parents can't come, agree on the questions beforehand so nothing gets lost.

3. The ten questions that get real answers

1. How is my child doing compared to grade-level expectations? This is the single most important question. Ontario grades run on Levels 1 to 4, and 'doing fine' can mean very different things. Ask where your child sits against the standard — not just against the class.

2. What are their biggest strengths right now? Start here on purpose. It tells you what to encourage at home, and it opens the teacher up before the harder questions.

3. Where are the gaps — and how big are they? A gap in September is normal. A gap the teacher describes as 'widening' or 'a concern' is not. Ask specifically about reading and math, since those two compound fastest.

4. How does my child participate in class? Quiet kids get overlooked, and struggling kids hide. Does your child ask questions? Volunteer answers? Seem engaged or checked out? This tells you things grades never will.

5. What do the learning skills tell you? The Ontario progress report rates responsibility, organization, independent work, collaboration, initiative, and self-regulation. Weak learning skills drag down every subject — and they're often the real story behind a disappointing grade.

6. Is homework coming back complete and on time? Homework habits in the fall predict report card results in the winter. If work is late or half-done now, that's a fixable problem — later, it becomes a pattern.

7. How is their reading level and math fluency, specifically? Vague answers like 'they're progressing' don't help you. Ask: are they reading at grade level? Do they know their math facts automatically? These are the two foundations everything else sits on.

8. Should I be concerned about anything socially or emotionally? Teachers see your child in a social setting you never witness. A quick check here can surface friendship issues, anxiety, or confidence problems early — when they're small.

9. What can we do at home over the next two months? This turns the meeting from a status update into a plan. The best teachers will give you two or three concrete actions, not 'just keep reading.'

10. When should we check in again — and what would trigger extra help? Agree on a follow-up date and a clear signal. 'If the next math unit test is below 70%, let's talk about support options' beats 'we'll see how it goes.'

4. What to do with the answers

Write down the two or three action items before you leave the school — you will forget them by the parking lot. Then do the simplest version of each one this week, not the perfect version someday. If the teacher flagged reading, twenty minutes of daily reading starts tonight. If math facts are shaky, five minutes of flashcard-style practice a day moves the needle fast. And if the gaps sound bigger than homework can fix, that is exactly what the fall checkpoint is for: catching it in October instead of discovering it on the February report card.

5. When the interview tells you it's time for help

There is a difference between 'a bit behind' and 'falling behind.' If the teacher uses words like 'concerned,' 'struggling,' or 'not meeting expectations' — or if the same issue came up last year — don't wait for the next report card to act. An Ontario-certified teacher can assess exactly where your child stands in a single session, target the specific gaps the interview surfaced, and get ahead of them while the school year is still young. October help is a tune-up; February help is a rescue.

Parent-teacher interviews only work if you walk in with questions and walk out with a plan. Ten good questions, fifteen minutes, and two or three action items — that's all it takes to turn a routine fall meeting into the most useful conversation of your child's school year.
