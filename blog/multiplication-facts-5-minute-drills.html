Multiplication Facts Still Not Sticking? Try These 5-Minute Drills

  • By Mr. Grewal
  • October 7, 2026
  • 6 Min

Five Minutes a Day Is All It Takes to Make Facts Automatic

If your child can work through a multiplication problem but freezes when asked "What's 7 × 8?" off the top of their head, you are looking at one of the most common — and most fixable — math frustrations of the elementary years. Multiplication facts need to become automatic, because every layer of math that follows — long division, fractions, algebra — assumes they are already there. The good news: five focused minutes a day beats an hour of weekend drilling. Here is how to make those five minutes count.

1. Why facts slip — and why that's normal

Children don't struggle with multiplication facts because they can't do math. They struggle because facts are memory, not logic — and memory only sticks with repeated, spaced recall. Studying a whole times table at once overloads working memory, so kids look at 7 × 8 a hundred times and still blank on it. The fix is to work in tiny sets, revisit them over days rather than cramming in one sitting, and always practise recalling the answer — not just reading it. Spaced practice is the same principle behind effective studying in older grades; starting it in Grade 3 or 4 builds a habit that pays off for a decade.

2. The 3-fact sprint

Pick three facts your child gets wrong most often — say, 6 × 7, 7 × 8, and 8 × 9. Write each on a card, answer side up first. Read them together once, then flip to the question side and rapid-fire for exactly two minutes. Miss one? Don't just say the answer — have your child say the whole fact out loud: "six times seven is forty-two." Hearing themselves say it cements it far better than you supplying the answer. Two minutes on, and the next day swap in up to two new facts while keeping one old one for review. Three facts a day, reviewed across the week, covers an entire times table in under a month.

3. Draw it, then say it

Kids remember pictures longer than strings of digits. For a fact that won't stick, grab graph paper and have your child draw the array — 7 × 8 becomes 7 rows of 8 squares. Count it together, then ask: "Where would you split this into two facts you already know?" Often they'll see 7 × 4 doubled, or 5 × 8 plus 2 × 8. Now the fact has a picture and a strategy attached, not just two lonely numbers. Students who can rebuild a forgotten fact from a strategy they understand are never truly stuck — and over time the picture fades and the answer arrives on its own.

4. Anchor facts and fact families

Nobody needs to memorize all 100 facts as separate items — half the table falls out of a handful of anchors. Teach the easy ones first: the 1s, 2s, 5s, and 10s, plus the doubles like 6 × 6. Then show how fact families connect: if your child knows 5 × 8 = 40, then 6 × 8 is just one more group of 8, or 42 + 8 if you go 6 × 7. The trick of "one more group" turns a forgotten fact into a small addition problem. Practise naming these connections out loud: "I know five eights, so six eights is forty-eight." Five minutes of this, and the table stops feeling like a wall.

5. The daily deal: same time, every day

The biggest reason drills fail is inconsistency, not the drills themselves. Anchor the five minutes to something that already happens: right after dinner, in the car on the way home, or while the microwave runs. Keep a small scorecard on the fridge — check off each day the drill happens. When your child sees a chain of checkmarks, they start protecting the streak themselves. A month of unbroken five-minute days transforms recall; a month of weekend-only marathons rarely does. Consistency is the entire secret.

6. Skip-counting speed tracks

Facts live inside skip-counting sequences, and most kids are shaky on at least one sequence — the 6s, 7s, 8s, or 9s. For one five-minute session, run a "speed track": you say the starting number, your child skip-counts to a target (by 7s up to 84) and back down. Time them once, then let them try to beat their own time tomorrow. The race-against-yourself format makes it a game instead of a test, and a solid 7s sequence quietly unlocks every 7 fact on the table. This is the drill kids ask to repeat.

7. How tutoring can help

If the facts still aren't sticking after a few weeks of steady drills, it often means something underneath needs attention — maybe the addition facts aren't solid, maybe the place-value understanding of what multiplication means never fully landed, or maybe your child has developed a "math anxiety" reflex that blanks them under any pressure. An Ontario-certified teacher can diagnose which of those is actually going on in a single session, then build the drill routine around your child's exact gaps instead of a generic table. That diagnosis is the difference between drilling harder and drilling smarter — and it usually only takes a few focused sessions to get things moving again.

Multiplication facts aren't a talent — they're a habit built five minutes at a time. Start tonight with three fact cards and a two-minute sprint, keep the same slot tomorrow, and watch what a month of consistency does. Your child doesn't need longer study sessions; they need the right reps, on the right facts, every single day.
